# Competitive Landscape: Dementia / Memory-Training / Reminiscence Products

**Domain:** Existing products — what exists, what works, where the gaps are
**Researcher note:** every substantive claim below carries a citation. Where I could not verify a company's claim against a primary source, I say so explicitly. Where the evidence is weak or negative, I say so plainly.
**Date compiled:** 2026-08-12

---

## Summary (10 bullets)

1. **Nobody has combined the four things we are proposing.** There are personalised-reminiscence apps (InspireD, GreyMatters, CIRCA), generic cognitive-training apps (BrainHQ, Lumosity, Constant Therapy), curated non-personal quiz apps (Memory Lane Games, My House of Memories), and clinician-facing assessment tools (Linus Health, Cogstate). **No product combines family-supplied personal content + a principled spaced-retrieval schedule + a longitudinal clinician/researcher view.** That is the genuine gap.
2. **The strongest evidence in the whole field is not for personalised content — it is for generic speed-of-processing training, and only in cognitively healthy older adults.** The ACTIVE 20-year follow-up found speed training *with boosters* cut claims-diagnosed dementia (adjusted HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.59–0.95); speed training *without* boosters did nothing (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.81–1.27), and memory and reasoning training arms were null ([Coe et al., 2026](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/trc2.70197)). This is a single trial, claims-based outcome, many comparisons, and one author consults for the company that sells the product ([NPR, 2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/02/18/nx-s1-5716010/brain-training-exercise-cut-dementia-risk-decades)).
3. **Cognitive training in people who already have mild-to-moderate dementia produces small cognitive gains and no functional or mood benefit.** Cochrane: global cognition SMD 0.42 (95% CI 0.23–0.62), moderate certainty vs. no-treatment control; but only 0.21 (95% CI −0.23–0.64), low certainty, vs. active control, and **no benefit on ADLs, mood, or behavioural symptoms** ([Bahar-Fuchs et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30909318/)). Nothing in this literature slows disease progression.
4. **Personalised reminiscence has better evidence for *relationship and wellbeing* outcomes than for memory.** InspireD improved mutuality, carer–patient relationship quality and WHO-5 wellbeing in people with dementia — but it was quasi-experimental with no control group ([Laird et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6231741/)). CIRCA's replicated finding is *more equal conversation*, not better memory ([Astell et al., Dundee REF case study](https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=35244)).
5. **The regulatory line is sharp and competitors have crossed it.** Lumosity paid $2m on a suspended $50m judgment for claiming its games "protect against dementia and Alzheimer's disease" ([FTC, 2016](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program)). A brain-training app was found in breach by the UK ASA purely for implying it could *assess* a condition, because it was not MHRA-registered ([ASA, GMRD Apps Ltd](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/gmrd-apps-ltd-a23-1215012-gmrd-apps-ltd.html)).
6. **The single most dangerous feature we could build is a score that looks like a screening result.** Under MHRA rules a product becomes a medical device if it diagnoses, monitors, or produces output that influences clinical decisions ([MHRA stand-alone software guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/64a7d22d7a4c230013bba33c/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps__including_IVDMDs_.pdf); [MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2019_11_guidance_en_0.pdf)). Our doctor/researcher dashboard is exactly where this risk lives.
7. **Prescription digital therapeutics as a business model has failed twice, publicly.** Pear Therapeutics went bankrupt; Akili abandoned its prescription model, laid off 40% of staff after posting $114k revenue against $15.3m expenses in a quarter, and later cut 46% more ([STAT, 2023](https://www.statnews.com/2023/09/13/akili-endeavorrx-prescription-business-model-lay-offs/); [STAT, 2024](https://www.statnews.com/2024/04/30/akili-interactive-digital-therapeutics-firm-announce-layoffs-restructuring/)). Payers do not reimburse these.
8. **The existing app estate is bad on privacy — which is our opening, because we are asking families to upload the most sensitive content imaginable.** Across 44 studies / 50 researched dementia apps: 9% discussed security features, 59% required personal information, 2% mentioned user privacy, and **none** specified data retention or deletion rights ([Ye et al., 2023, JMIR 25:e46188](https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e46188)).
9. **Engagement, not efficacy, is where dementia apps die.** Independent app-quality review of dementia apps scored engagement lowest of all uMARS dimensions (3.04/5, vs functionality 3.76) ([Chen et al., 2024, JMIR Form Res 8:e51076](https://formative.jmir.org/2024/1/e51076)). The iCST app trial found participants finished the content faster than expected and needed more of it ([Rai et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35221680/)).
10. **The best-designed competitor for our patient surface is Memory Lane Games, and its key design decision is that there is no scoring and no time limit** ([memorylanegames.com](https://www.memorylanegames.com/)). It is also the closest to us commercially ($9.99/mo consumer subscription) — but it uses a curated stock library, *not* family photos. That is the seam we can enter through.

## Key findings with citations

### A. Generic brain-training incumbents

#### Lumosity (Lumos Labs)
- **What it does:** ~40 casual games claimed to train specific brain areas; consumer subscription.
- **Evidence claims and whether they hold up:** They do not. The FTC alleged Lumosity claimed its games would "delay memory decline; protect against dementia and Alzheimer's disease," plus unsubstantiated claims for ADHD, PTSD, stroke and TBI. Lumos Labs settled for $2m in redress against a **$50m suspended judgment**, and was required to obtain **human clinical testing before making a broad range of cognition claims**. It was also cited for failing to disclose that consumer testimonials had been solicited via contests offering a free iPad, a lifetime subscription and a trip to San Francisco. ([FTC press release, 2016](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program); [FTC case file 132-3212](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/132-3212-lumos-labs-inc-lumosity-mobile-online-cognitive-game); [FTC business blog, "Mind the gap"](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2016/01/mind-gap-what-lumosity-promised-vs-what-it-could-prove); summarised at [Olshan Advertising Law Blog](https://www.olshanlaw.com/Advertising-Law-Blog/Luminosity-FTC-Charges-Deceptive-Advertising))
- **Evidence class:** (d) disproven / unsubstantiated as marketed.
- **Business model:** B2C subscription with auto-renewal — the auto-renewal cancellation friction was itself part of the FTC order.
- **Lesson for us:** the enforcement hook was not "the games don't work," it was "you had no competent and reliable scientific evidence for the *specific* claim you made." Claim discipline is a product requirement, not a marketing afterthought.

#### BrainHQ / Posit Science
- **What it does:** ~29 exercises, heavily weighted to speed-of-processing ("Double Decision", derived from the Useful Field of View task). B2C subscription plus B2B2C via Medicare Advantage plans and insurers.
- **Evidence claims:** Posit Science markets "over 100 published studies" and, since February 2026, a headline claim that speed training cuts dementia risk 25% ([BrainHQ ACTIVE page](https://www.brainhq.com/world-class-science/information-researchers/active-study/); [BrainHQ, "Breakthrough…"](https://www.brainhq.com/better-brain-health/article/brain-news/breakthrough-in-brain-health-training/)).
- **Do they hold up? Partly — and with sharp caveats.** The underlying paper is real and NIH-funded: in the 20-year ACTIVE follow-up, dementia was ascertained from Medicare claims using the Chronic Conditions Warehouse ICD algorithm (1999–2019). Speed training **with boosters** gave adjusted HR 0.75 (95% CI 0.59–0.95); 105/264 (40%) diagnosed vs 239/491 (49%) in control. Speed training **without** boosters: HR 1.01 (95% CI 0.81–1.27). The booster-vs-no-booster contrast within the speed arm was borderline (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.66–1.00). Memory and reasoning training arms were null. Of 2,802 enrolled, 2,021 were in the analytic (traditional-Medicare) sample. ([Coe et al., 2026, *Alzheimer's & Dementia: TRCI*](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/trc2.70197); [Johns Hopkins Medicine release](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/news/newsroom/news-releases/2026/02/cognitive-speed-training-linked-to-lower-dementia-incidence-up-to-20-years-later); [summary of statistics](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260302/Can-brain-training-prevent-dementia-Long-term-trial-shows-speed-training-with-boosters-makes-a-difference.aspx))
- **Honest reading:** this is (b) *promising but fragile*, not (a) proven. Reported criticisms: >20 statistical tests were run raising false-positive risk (Sarah Ackley, Brown University, as reported by [NPR, 2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/02/18/nx-s1-5716010/brain-training-exercise-cut-dementia-risk-decades)); one study author (Karlene Ball) co-developed the training and consults for Posit Science; the outcome is *claims-coded diagnosis*, which is sensitive to healthcare utilisation and coding behaviour — plausibly itself affected by being in an intervention arm. The authors themselves flag claims-based misclassification and exclusion of Medicare Advantage enrollees as limitations.
- **Critical for us:** the population was **cognitively healthy community-dwelling 65+**, not MCI and not dementia. The result does **not** license any claim about our users. And the effective ingredient was *speed of visual processing under load* — a mechanic that is nearly the opposite of a gentle, personalised, error-tolerant reminiscence exercise.

#### Constant Therapy (Constant Therapy Health)
- **What it does:** clinician-prescribed and self-serve speech/language/cognitive exercise sets, adaptive difficulty ("NeuroPerformance Engine"), used mainly in post-stroke aphasia, TBI, and increasingly MCI/dementia. ([constanttherapyhealth.com/science](https://constanttherapyhealth.com/science/))
- **Evidence:** genuinely the strongest clinical publication record of the consumer-adjacent players — including a virtual RCT in post-stroke aphasia (Braley et al., 2021, *Frontiers in Neurology*), a dose-frequency study (Cordella et al., 2022, *JMIR*), retrospective home-vs-clinic cohorts (Godlove et al., 2019), and a **feasibility RCT of home-based electronic cognitive therapy in Alzheimer's disease** ([Marin et al., 2022, *JMIR Formative Research* 6(9):e34450](https://formative.jmir.org/2022/9/e34450)). Aphasia work reports ~70% compliance over 10–20 weeks. A dementia telepractice paper appears in ASHA Perspectives ([2021_PERSP-20-00123](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_PERSP-20-00123)) and an efficacy/utility review in ([2025_PERSP-24-00260](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2025_PERSP-24-00260)).
- **Regulatory status — important nuance:** they hold an **FDA Breakthrough Device *designation*** for a speech therapy app. A designation is an expedited-review pathway, **not** a clearance or approval. Marketing that blurs the two is a live risk area, and we should never imitate it.
- **Evidence class:** (b) promising, adequately powered only in aphasia; (c) plausible in dementia.
- **Business model:** tiered subscription — patient, clinician, institution.

### B. Reminiscence and life-story products (the closest neighbours)

#### CIRCA (Computer Interactive Reminiscence and Conversation Aid) — Universities of Dundee & St Andrews
- **What it does:** touchscreen multimedia (photos, video, music) as a *conversation scaffold* between a person with dementia and a carer. Deliberately non-testing: the user browses, they don't answer.
- **Evidence — and this is the most design-relevant finding in the whole survey:** compared with traditional reminiscence methods, CIRCA produced more successful conversation, with people with dementia **more likely to initiate** conversation and being **more equal partners** in it. Deployed across NHS, council, Age Concern, Alzheimer Scotland and private care settings. ([Dundee REF impact case study](https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=35244); [Dundee Discovery](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/using-a-touch-screen-computer-to-support-relationships-between-pe))
- **Recent replication:** Canadian feasibility testing — 69 clients with dementia, 19 staff, 4 family carers, 49 sessions across six community sites plus four homes; session length 6–82 min, mean 32.25 min ([Astell, Dove, Green et al., 2025, *Alzheimer's & Dementia* 21(S4):e104889](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12742341/)).
- **Note the huge variance in session length (6 to 82 minutes).** A fixed "10-minute daily session" is a designer's fantasy; real sessions vary by an order of magnitude.
- **Evidence class:** (a/b) proven for conversation quality, feasibility-level for anything else. **No memory outcome claimed.**
- **Gap it leaves:** CIRCA is generic-era content in institutional settings, is not a consumer product, and has no scheduling engine or longitudinal measurement.

#### InspireD (Ulster University + Dementia NI) — *the single most relevant precedent for us*
- **What it does:** a tablet app where **families upload their own photos, video and audio** to build a personal digital memory book, used in a structured 12-week personalised-reminiscence programme with a carer.
- **Evidence:** quasi-experimental, 30 dyads (60 participants, 58 retained), early-to-moderate dementia, ages 61–94, measured at baseline / week 13 / week 19. People with dementia improved significantly on mutuality (3.24 → 3.64, p<.001), Quality of Carer–Patient Relationship (58.07 → 63.2, p<.001) and WHO-5 wellbeing (60.8 → 70.7, p<.001). **Carers showed non-significant change on all measures.** ([Laird EA, Ryan A, McCauley C, et al., 2018, *JMIR Ment Health* 5(3):e57](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6231741/))
- **Honest limitations, stated by the authors:** no control group, cannot establish causation, likely Hawthorne effect, women with dementia under-represented.
- **Usability work:** a co-created follow-up with eye-gaze analysis confirmed the minimalist design ([PMC8369094](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8369094/)). App released publicly 2021 ([Ulster University](https://www.ulster.ac.uk/news/2021/march/ulster-university-launches-new-app-for-people-living-with-dementia); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/inspired-dementia-reminiscence/id1544800098)).
- **Evidence class:** (b) promising but underpowered and uncontrolled.
- **Gap it leaves:** it is a *browsing* app. There is no retrieval practice, no schedule, no adaptive difficulty, no clinician view, and no commercial model — it is a research output, effectively abandonware-adjacent.

#### GreyMatters
- **What it does:** iPad "interactive life storybook" — families upload photos with short text and **voice narration**, plus music and light games. Grew out of an MFA thesis (Jenny Rozbruch, School of Visual Arts, 2013). Free, iOS-only. ([greymatterstous.com](https://www.greymatterstous.com/); [HuffPost, 2015](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/grey-matters-app_n_6572048); [Dementia Action Alliance app directory](https://daanow.org/discovery-center/engagement-socialization/apps/))
- **Evidence:** essentially none of consequence — design-award recognition ([The Index Project](https://theindexproject.org/award/nominees/2996)) and a small write-up, not controlled trials.
- **Evidence class:** (c) plausible mechanism only.
- **Why it matters:** it is the closest existing thing to "family uploads photos + voice of relatives" and it never achieved scale. The reason is instructive and appears repeatedly across this category: **the content-entry burden falls entirely on an unsupported family member, once, with no ongoing reason to return.**

#### My House of Memories (National Museums Liverpool)
- **What it does:** free app of museum objects across decades with multimedia, saveable to a "memory tree"/"memory box"/timeline. Co-created with people with dementia via Mersey Care and Innovate Dementia; extended with an LGBTQ+ content collection; supported by a tablet loan service. ([National Museums Liverpool](https://www.liverpoolmuseums.org.uk/house-of-memories/my-house-of-memories-app); [Museums+Heritage](https://museumsandheritage.com/advisor/posts/national-museums-liverpools-dementia-app-expands-for-lgbtq-community/))
- **Business model:** grant/museum funded, free at point of use. Not a company. Sustainable only because a public institution absorbs the cost.
- **Evidence:** programme-level and qualitative; I found no controlled evaluation of the app itself.
- **Evidence class:** (c).
- **Design lesson worth stealing:** cultural specificity is a first-class feature, not a nicety. Generic 1950s stock content fails people whose 1950s looked different — hence their LGBTQ+ collection. Our family-supplied-content model solves this natively, and that is a real differentiator.

#### Memory Lane Games — *our closest commercial competitor*
- **What it does:** 3,000+ deliberately simple picture quizzes on themes (music, food, pets, places, cars). Explicitly **"no time-limits or scoring"** and designed to avoid failure experiences. Marketed for reminiscence, conversation, and redirection from sundowning/wandering. ([memorylanegames.com](https://www.memorylanegames.com/); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/memory-lane-games/id1517193093); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.memorylanegames.memorylanegameslite))
- **Business model:** **$9.99 / £9.99 per month**, 14-day free trial, offline play. Consumer-direct.
- **Evidence claims — and a caution:** their site reports caregiver-survey figures ("67% of caregivers felt the app made them happier", "66% believed time spent was worthwhile"). Third-party coverage has rendered similar figures as coming from "a randomized controlled trial" (e.g. "92% of caregivers felt more relaxed"), which I could not trace to any published trial. **The numbers drift between sources and the study design label appears to be applied loosely.** Treat as (c) plausible mechanism, marketing-grade evidence.
- **Recognition:** Mayo Clinic / ASU MedTech Accelerator winner; Longitude Prize semi-finalist; Google Play #WeArePlay feature ([Android Developers Blog, 2025](https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2025/03/weareplay-how-memory-lane-games-helps-people-with-dementia.html)).
- **The gap they leave wide open:** the content is a **curated stock library, not the user's own life.** They have proven a family will pay ~$10/month for gentle, frustration-free picture quizzes. They have *not* solved personalisation.

#### MindMate
- **What it does:** a multi-feature app — brain games, exercise videos, nutrition, a "jukebox" music feature, a TV section, and a reminder/prospective-memory tool.
- **Evidence:** a single-case experimental design study of the **reminder** feature only: four participants with Alzheimer's identified, three recruited, **two completed**. Both gave positive usability ratings and intended to continue. ([McGoldrick C, Crawford S, Evans JJ, 2021, *Neuropsychological Rehabilitation* 31(1)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09602011.2019.1653936); [full text, University of Glasgow eprints](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/192827/7/192827.pdf))
- **Evidence class:** (c) — n=2 is a case series, not evidence of effect. The company's "science" page also references Parkinson's work ([mindmate-app.com/science](https://www.mindmate-app.com/science)).
- **Lesson:** MindMate is the archetype of the **kitchen-sink dementia app** — games + diet + exercise + social + reminders. The systematic review data suggests this breadth is not what fails; 60% of researched dementia apps are *single-feature*, which the reviewers criticise. But breadth without depth produced an app whose only published evidence covers one minor feature.

#### Rendever (VR reminiscence for senior living)
- **What it does:** group VR in senior-living communities; family members can place a resident virtually in a childhood home, wedding venue etc. B2B sale to senior living operators and home care.
- **Evidence:** NIA STTR grants and a ~$4.5m NIH award; a UCSB-partnered study comparing VR family engagement with video calls reports improved relationship quality (p=0.006), social network (p=0.03), relationship satisfaction (p=0.005), reminiscence (p<0.001) and positive wellbeing (p<0.001); a pilot reported 60% stable-or-improved loneliness and 64% stable-or-improved apathy at 3 months. **Their own research page states results are "actively being analyzed for scientific publication" — i.e. these headline p-values are not yet peer-reviewed.** ([Rendever research](https://www.rendever.com/research/largest-clinical-trial-on-vr-for-dementia); [family engagement study](https://www.rendever.com/research/vr-family-engagement-part-1); [McKnight's Senior Living](https://www.mcknightsseniorliving.com/news/clinical-trial-aims-to-determine-if-virtual-reality-can-compete-with-senior-living-in-addressing-social-isolation/))
- **Evidence class:** (b) at best; currently unpublished. Note also that p-values without effect sizes across six-plus outcomes is precisely the multiple-comparison pattern criticised in the ACTIVE coverage.
- **Practical caution:** VR headsets in dementia carry real risks — disorientation, nausea, falls, and inability to remove the headset. Rendever's model requires staff facilitation, which is why it is B2B-only. **We should not go near head-mounted displays.**

#### Tovertafel ("Magic Table", Tover / formerly Active Cues)
- **What it does:** ceiling-mounted projector casting interactive light games onto a table; designed for **moderate to severe** dementia in long-term care, where screens and tablets fail. Product of a four-year PhD on reducing passivity. ([tover.care](https://www.tover.care/us/tovertafel/seniors-dementia); [Tovertafel research](https://tovertafel.com/research/research-on-dementia/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tovertafel))
- **Evidence:** paired-sample t-tests showed significantly fewer memory-related behaviours and less apathy during intervention vs control periods ([PMC10739036](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10739036/)).
- **Evidence class:** (b), small.
- **Why it is in this list:** it is the clearest demonstration that **product form must follow disease stage.** Tovertafel exists because tablets stop working at moderate-severe dementia. Our product is inherently an early-stage product; we should say so rather than pretend otherwise.

### C. Research vehicles and assessment tools (adjacent, and a regulatory warning)

#### Sea Hero Quest
- **What it was:** a free mobile navigation game built by Glitchers with UCL, UEA, Alzheimer's Research UK and Deutsche Telekom, purely as a **data-collection instrument**. ~4.3 million players, generating the equivalent of thousands of years of lab time. Results include a global benchmark for spatial navigation, GDP/gender-inequality correlates of navigation ability, and — most importantly — the finding that people at genetic risk of Alzheimer's (APOE-ε4 carriers) can be distinguished from non-carriers on specific game levels ([Coughlan et al., via UCL, 2019](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2019/apr/mobile-game-can-detect-alzheimers-risk); [Alzheimer's Research UK](https://www.alzheimersresearchuk.org/research/for-researchers/resources-and-information/sea-hero-quest/); [Deutsche Telekom](https://www.telekom.com/en/media/media-information/archive/analysis-of-data-collected-by-sea-hero-quest-534272))
- **Critical detail:** **the consumer version was withdrawn from app stores when the project concluded.** It now exists only as a bespoke research platform. It was never intended to help the individual playing it, and it did not.
- **Evidence class:** (a) as a research instrument; (n/a) as a therapy — it never claimed to be one, which is exactly why it never got into trouble.
- **Lesson:** a research-first product that is honest about being research-first can achieve enormous scale without regulatory exposure. Our doctor/researcher surface should be framed this way.

#### Linus Health (DCTclock), Cogstate (Cognigram), Cambridge Cognition (CANTAB Mobile), Cognivue
- **What they do:** clinician-facing digital cognitive assessment. DCTclock digitises the clock-drawing test, extracting 700+ features via ML; it is described as an **FDA-registered Class II device** (the pen-based variant being 510(k)-exempt) ([Linus Health](https://linushealth.com/about-assessments)). Cognigram is a **Class II 510(k)-exempt** digital medical device with marketing authorisation in multiple jurisdictions, for ages 6–99, in clinic or at home ([Cogstate](https://www.cogstate.com/healthcare/cognigram/)). CANTAB Mobile, ANAM, CognICA and Cognivue are also FDA-cleared/registered assessment tools ([FDA-approved digital cognitive assessment tools review](https://c.peerview.com/curriculum/programs/150209710/pa/FDA-Approved_Digital_Cognitive_Assessment_Tools_PA21_V1.pdf)).
- **Validation caveat:** a 2025 systematic review assessed digital cognitive assessments against the FDA BEST framework and found the validation status uneven across contexts of use ([Porta-Mas et al., 2025, *Alzheimer's & Dementia*](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/alz.70753)).
- **Business model:** B2B — health systems, physician practices, pharma trials, sports/schools.
- **Why this matters enormously for us:** these companies went through device registration **specifically because they output a clinical interpretation of cognition.** The moment our doctor dashboard shows anything that reads as "this patient's cognition is declining," we are functionally in their category — and unlike them, we would be there without a regulatory file.

### D. Business-model graveyard

- **Akili Interactive (EndeavorRx):** first FDA-cleared prescription video-game therapeutic (ADHD). Q2 2023: **$114,000 revenue against $15.3m expenses.** September 2023: abandoned the prescription model, cut 40% of staff, eliminated field sales and market access. 2024: EndeavorOTC cleared for OTC adult ADHD, sales still slumped, further 46% workforce cut, "evaluating strategic alternatives." Only ~25% of payers said they would cover prescription digital therapeutics. ([STAT, 2023](https://www.statnews.com/2023/09/13/akili-endeavorrx-prescription-business-model-lay-offs/); [STAT, 2024](https://www.statnews.com/2024/04/30/akili-interactive-digital-therapeutics-firm-announce-layoffs-restructuring/); [Behavioral Health Business](https://bhbusiness.com/2023/09/13/akili-bails-on-prescription-digital-therapeutic-route-lays-off-40-of-staff/); [Healthcare Brew](https://www.healthcare-brew.com/stories/2023/09/15/digital-therapeutics-company-akili-ditches-prescription-business-model))
- **Pear Therapeutics:** bankrupt 2023 for the same reason — payer coverage never materialised (referenced in the same STAT/Healthcare Brew coverage).
- **Read-through:** FDA clearance is not a business model. If we ever pursue device status, it must be because we intend to *sell to a payer or a health system that will actually pay*, not because clearance sounds impressive.

| **ASA (UK)** | Neuronix (NeuroAD) | Upheld — advertised as "a new treatment for Alzheimer's disease" with memory/mood/ADL/attention benefits. ASA reviewed published *and unpublished* papers submitted by the company and concluded the evidence base was insufficient | [Alzheimer's Society, 2025](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/news/2025-10-08/complaint-upheld-against-advertising-neuroad-alzheimers-disease-treatment) |
| **ASA/MHRA (UK), general** | Wellness brands | ASA has drawn a hard line that claims falling within MHRA-regulated territory cannot be made by unregistered products, regardless of framing | [CMS Law-Now, 2025](https://cms-lawnow.com/en/ealerts/2025/12/wellness-brands-under-scrutiny-asa-draws-a-hard-line-on-mhra-regulated-claims) |

**When does a "memory app" become a regulated medical device?**

- **UK (MHRA):** software is a device if it diagnoses, prevents, monitors or treats a condition, provides clinical decisions/recommendations that influence treatment, or substitutes for a healthcare professional's judgment. Out of scope: **simple data storage/record-keeping without analysis, general communication between users, and general wellbeing content.** The MHRA extended this explicitly to digital mental health technologies from February 2025, with a risk-tiered classification: mood-tracking with no medical advice ≈ Class I; AI that adapts therapy exercises based on user responses ≈ Class IIa/IIb. ([MHRA stand-alone software guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/64a7d22d7a4c230013bba33c/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps__including_IVDMDs_.pdf); [MHRA digital mental health position summarised](https://gosuperscript.com/news-and-resources/new-rules-for-digital-mental-health-apps/))
  - ⚠️ **Note the Class IIa/IIb trigger: "adapts exercises based on user responses."** A spaced-repetition scheduler *is* an algorithm that adapts content based on user responses. Framed as therapy for a condition, that is squarely a device. Framed as an engagement/activity scheduler with no medical claim, it is not. **The framing does the regulatory work.**
- **EU (MDR):** qualification and classification per [MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2019_11_guidance_en_0.pdf), classification via Annex VIII **Rule 11** — software providing information used for diagnostic/therapeutic decisions is at minimum Class IIa, escalating with the severity of consequence. The revised guidance adds decision trees and explicit treatment of borderline wellness apps and modular/compound functionality.
- **US (FDA):** the 2026 final General Wellness guidance applies a two-part test — (1) is the intended use general wellness (maintaining health / reducing risk of chronic disease through lifestyle), and (2) is it low-risk? Intended use is assessed **objectively from labelling, marketing and statements**, not from what you privately intend. Device territory includes disease-specific or diagnostic language, claims guiding clinical management, ongoing monitoring for medical treatment, and outputs labelled "abnormal"/"pathological." Wellness territory permits generic "consult your healthcare provider" prompts **without disease references.** Notably, FDA's own example set states that a claim to *improve memory* is a general wellness claim — it was the *electrical stimulation hardware* that made a memory-claiming neurostimulator non-low-risk. ([FDA General Wellness guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/90652/download); [Troutman Pepper Locke summary of the 2026 final guidance](https://www.troutman.com/insights/fdas-2026-guidance-on-general-wellness-devices-policy-for-low-risk-devices/); [Ropes & Gray alert](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/01/fda-adapts-with-the-times-on-digital-health-updated-guidances-on-general-wellness-products))

**Our three specific regulatory exposures, ranked:**
1. **The doctor/researcher dashboard.** Any longitudinal cognitive metric presented to a clinician is "information used for clinical decisions." Highest risk. (MDR Rule 11; MHRA "influences clinical decisions".)
2. **Any onboarding assessment or baseline test** used to set difficulty. If it looks like a screen, it is a screen — see the ASA/Impulse ruling, which was upheld on an *implied* assessment.
3. **Marketing copy.** The Lumosity order and the FDA "intended use is judged from your marketing" principle mean the landing page can create a regulated device out of an unregulated product without a line of code changing.

**Populations must not be blurred.** ACTIVE = cognitively healthy. Cochrane cognitive training = mild-to-moderate dementia. InspireD/CIRCA = early-to-moderate dementia in dyads. Tovertafel = moderate-to-severe in institutions. **Our app targets MCI to mild dementia living at home with an involved family member.** For that specific population, the honest statement is: *reminiscence and structured engagement have good evidence for wellbeing and relationship quality, weak-to-moderate evidence for small cognitive gains on trained material, and no evidence for changing the disease.*

## What has been tried and failed, and why

1. **Free, grant-funded, research-built reminiscence apps (InspireD, GreyMatters, My House of Memories).** They demonstrate the mechanism, then stall. Failure mode: **no revenue → no content pipeline → no maintenance → the family does 100% of the content work once and never returns.**
2. **Generic brain-training marketed at dementia fear (Lumosity).** Failure mode: **the claim outran the evidence and the regulator arrived.**
3. **Prescription digital therapeutics (Akili, Pear).** Failure mode: **payers wouldn't reimburse.** Clearance cost millions and bought nothing commercially.
4. **Kitchen-sink dementia apps (MindMate).** Failure mode: breadth with no depth; only one minor feature ever got evaluated, in n=2.
5. **Hardware-dependent interventions (Rendever VR, Tovertafel).** Not failures — but both are locked into B2B institutional sales because they need staff facilitation and capital equipment. Neither reaches the family at home in the early stage.
6. **Research-only instruments (Sea Hero Quest).** Enormous scale, zero individual benefit, and withdrawn from consumer stores when funding ended. Honest, but it helped no patient.

## The genuine unmet gap

Stated precisely, in one paragraph:

> There is currently **no product that takes family-supplied personal content (photos, names, life events, relatives' voices), turns it into short daily retrieval-practice sessions scheduled by a principled algorithm adapted for a declining memory, keeps the caregiver's content-entry burden low and ongoing rather than high and one-off, and gives a clinician or researcher a longitudinal, privacy-respecting view across individuals — while making no claim to treat or slow disease.**

Four sub-gaps, each independently defensible:

- **Personalisation gap.** Memory Lane Games proved families pay ~$10/month for gentle picture quizzes, but its content is stock. InspireD/GreyMatters proved personal content moves wellbeing outcomes, but they are browsers with no practice loop and no business model. **Nobody has joined those two halves.**
- **Scheduling gap.** Spaced retrieval has the strongest direct-memory evidence in dementia and is delivered today by speech-language pathologists by hand, on paper. There is no consumer product implementing it properly over personal content. (Note honestly: SM-2/FSRS were designed for *acquisition* by intact memory systems with expanding intervals over months. In progressive impairment the retention curve moves the wrong way over time, and intervals may need to *contract*. This is a genuine research contribution, and also a genuine risk.)
- **Caregiver-burden gap.** Every app in this survey puts a one-time, high-effort content-entry task on a family member who is already exhausted. Nobody has designed the *content pipeline* — distributed contribution across multiple relatives, drip-fed prompts, voice capture from a phone call — as the primary product surface.
- **Measurement gap.** The clinician/researcher tools (Linus, Cogstate) are episodic in-clinic snapshots. The engagement products collect rich daily interaction data and throw it away. Longitudinal, ecologically-valid, consented, individual-and-aggregate data is the thing a researcher genuinely cannot buy today. **This is the most defensible long-term asset in the whole design — and the most regulated.**

## DIRECT DESIGN IMPLICATIONS

### Claims and framing (do this first — it constrains everything else)
- **DO** frame the product as: *connection, conversation, engagement, and enjoyment built from your own life* — outcomes for which the evidence is genuinely positive (CIRCA, InspireD).
- **NEVER** claim, imply, or allow marketing copy to suggest: slows/prevents/delays dementia or Alzheimer's; improves memory generally; reduces dementia risk; is a treatment. The FTC order against Lumos Labs and the ASA ruling against Neuronix are both exactly this.
- **NEVER** use incentivised testimonials without disclosure — this was a separate FTC count against Lumosity ([Olshan summary](https://www.olshanlaw.com/Advertising-Law-Blog/Luminosity-FTC-Charges-Deceptive-Advertising)).
- **DO** write the claims boundary into a document that gates marketing copy, app-store listings, and the doctor dashboard's label text. Under FDA's 2026 guidance intended use is judged from marketing, so this is an engineering constraint, not a comms preference.
- **DO** state plainly in-product: "This app does not treat or slow dementia."

### Patient surface
- **NEVER** show a score, a percentage correct, a streak that can break, a countdown timer, or a red "wrong" state. Memory Lane Games' explicit "no time-limits or scoring" is the most validated design decision in the consumer category, and Cochrane's null result on mood/behaviour means we get no credit for pushing harder.
- **DO** treat a failed retrieval as a **cue to reveal**, not a failure event — i.e. degrade gracefully into recognition, then into simple viewing with a caption. The person should never end a session having been shown they are worse than last month.
- **NEVER** present anything the person could interpret as a test of whether their dementia is progressing. This is the most plausible route to real distress in our product.
- **DO** design for a session length that varies wildly. CIRCA sessions ran **6 to 82 minutes** ([Astell et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12742341/)). A hard-coded "10 minutes then stop" is wrong in both directions.
- **DO** design for a *companion present*. Both InspireD and CIRCA are dyadic. The strongest outcomes in this literature are relational, and a solo-play design forfeits them.
- **NEVER** include photographs of, or questions about, deceased relatives without an explicit caregiver-set flag. Repeated re-discovery of a bereavement is a well-known harm in reminiscence work and our scheduler would actively cause it on a recurring schedule. **The spaced-repetition engine is the mechanism by which this harm becomes systematic.** Treat "sensitive/deceased/estranged" as a first-class content attribute with scheduler suppression.
- **DO** allow the caregiver to permanently retire any item instantly, from the patient's device, mid-session, without explanation.
- **NEVER** ship head-mounted VR. Disorientation, falls, and inability to self-remove the headset are why Rendever is staff-facilitated and institution-only.

### Scheduling engine
- **DO** implement spaced retrieval — it has the best direct-memory evidence in this population and beats errorless learning for face–name pairs ([Haslam et al., 2011](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13803395.2010.533155)).
- **DO** combine it with errorless presentation (show the answer before asking, prevent guessing), since the two are routinely delivered together in clinical practice ([spaced retrieval + errorless learning trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26504282/)).
- **DO** allow intervals to **contract, not only expand.** Off-the-shelf SM-2/FSRS assume monotonic improvement. Our users' underlying retention declines. An unmodified SM-2 will push intervals out just as ability falls, guaranteeing an accelerating failure rate — the exact experience we must avoid.
- **DO NOT** let the scheduler ever produce a visible "difficulty" or "memory strength" number in the patient UI. Internally fine; externally it is a progression meter.
- **DO** note that ACTIVE's *only* positive arm was the one with **booster sessions** ([Coe et al., 2026](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/trc2.70197)). Whatever else is uncertain, distributed reinforcement over years beat a single training block. That is at least directionally consistent with a spacing engine.

### Caregiver surface (the real product)
- **DO** make content contribution the primary designed experience, not an onboarding chore. Every failed competitor in this category loaded 100% of content work onto one exhausted person, once.
- **DO** support multi-contributor content — siblings, grandchildren, a niece who has the wedding photos — with lightweight invite links. The voice recordings from relatives in our concept are the single most differentiated asset; no surveyed product captures them systematically (GreyMatters supports narration but only from the primary user).
- **DO** drip-feed prompts ("Do you have a photo of the house on Elm Street?") rather than presenting an empty library.
- **DO** show the caregiver *engagement and enjoyment* data (did they play, did they smile, what did they talk about) rather than performance data. Cochrane found no functional/mood benefit from cognitive training; if we report performance we are reporting decline, and we will make caregivers miserable and possibly distort care.

### Doctor / researcher surface — the regulated surface
- **NEVER** display a metric that reads as a cognitive status, screening result, or trajectory of decline. That is MDR Rule 11 territory and MHRA "influences clinical decisions" territory (see [MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2019_11_guidance_en_0.pdf); [MHRA guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/64a7d22d7a4c230013bba33c/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps__including_IVDMDs_.pdf)).
- **DO** frame it as **engagement and usage analytics for research**, explicitly labelled "not a clinical assessment; not for diagnosis or treatment decisions." Sea Hero Quest is the model: an honest research instrument attracted millions of users and zero enforcement.
- **DO NOT** ship any onboarding "baseline assessment." If we need difficulty calibration, derive it silently from in-session behaviour. The ASA upheld against Impulse Brain Training for an *implied* diagnostic test on a Facebook ad ([ASA ruling](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/gmrd-apps-ltd-a23-1215012-gmrd-apps-ltd.html)).
- **DO** get an explicit written regulatory position early — if we later want a clinician-facing cognitive metric, that is a deliberate decision to become a Class IIa device, with the cost and the payer question that Akili and Pear failed to answer.

### Privacy — our clearest competitive advantage
The published state of the art is dismal: 9% of researched dementia apps discussed security features, 59% required personal information, 2% mentioned user privacy, **none** specified data retention periods or deletion rights ([Ye et al., 2023, JMIR 25:e46188](https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e46188)).
- **DO** ship, from v1: a plain-language privacy policy, explicit retention periods, one-click export, one-click delete-everything, per-contributor consent, and a separate consent for research use that is genuinely optional and revocable.
- **DO** handle **capacity and consent** explicitly. The patient may not be able to consent to research use of their own life story. Design a proxy-consent + assent model and record who consented to what, when, and on what basis.
- **DO** treat family photographs and relatives' voice recordings as special-category-adjacent data with a documented lawful basis. This is a differentiator we can market honestly — unlike efficacy.

### Product/UX generally
- **DO** use tablets. 61% of researched dementia apps did ([Ye et al., 2023](https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e46188)).
- **DO NOT** use the System Usability Scale to validate with people with dementia — reviewers flagged it as unsuitable, and think-aloud protocols overload working memory and produce inaccurate results ([Ye et al., 2023](https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e46188)). Use observation of the dyad and video-coded engagement instead, as CIRCA did.
- **DO** plan for content exhaustion. The iCST app trial found participants completed activities faster than anticipated and the authors concluded content expansion was necessary to sustain engagement ([Rai et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35221680/)). Our personal-content model is a partial answer, but a family has a finite photo album — plan generative reuse (same photo, different question framings) and ongoing contribution prompts.
- **DO** treat cultural and biographical specificity as core. National Museums Liverpool had to build a separate LGBTQ+ collection because generic nostalgia excludes people ([Museums+Heritage](https://museumsandheritage.com/advisor/posts/national-museums-liverpools-dementia-app-expands-for-lgbtq-community/)). Our model gets this for free — say so.

### Business model
- **DO** price B2C at the level Memory Lane Games has already validated (~$9.99/month with a free trial) — a family caregiver is the payer, the patient is the user, and there is demonstrated willingness to pay.
- **DO NOT** build toward payer reimbursement or a prescription model. Only ~25% of payers would cover prescription digital therapeutics; Pear went bankrupt and Akili abandoned the model ([STAT, 2023](https://www.statnews.com/2023/09/13/akili-endeavorrx-prescription-business-model-lay-offs/)).
- **DO** consider the research/clinical-trial data channel (Cogstate/Sea Hero Quest style) as the second revenue line, but only with a clean consent architecture built in from day one — retrofitting consent onto existing data is not possible.
- **DO** make auto-renewal cancellation trivially easy. It was part of the FTC order against Lumosity and is now standard enforcement territory.

## Open questions

1. **Does personal content actually improve *retrieval* outcomes, or only affect and engagement?** InspireD and CIRCA measured relationships and wellbeing, not memory. Nobody has run the head-to-head: personal photos vs. stock photos in a retrieval-practice paradigm. This is genuinely unknown and is a publishable pilot question for us.
2. **Do expanding intervals work at all when the underlying retention curve is falling?** The entire SM-2/FSRS lineage assumes improvement. I found no product and no trial that has tested a contracting-interval scheduler in progressive impairment. Highest-value and highest-risk unknown in the design.
3. **What is the real dropout curve?** Feasibility studies report flattering short-window adherence (iCST: 77% ≥20 min/week over 11 weeks). Nobody in this survey reports 12-month retention in a home setting. Assume it is bad and design content contribution to be the retention mechanism.
4. **Where exactly is the MHRA/FDA line for a dyadic engagement app with an adaptive scheduler?** The MHRA's own example places "AI chatbot offering therapy exercises based on user responses" at Class IIa/IIb. An adaptive spaced-repetition engine is uncomfortably close in mechanism and far away in claim. This needs a written regulatory opinion before the doctor dashboard is built, not after.
5. **How do we handle the moment the person stops being able to do the exercise?** Every product here targets a stage band and none handles the transition out of it (Tovertafel exists precisely because tablets stop working). What does our app become at moderate dementia — does it degrade into a CIRCA-style browsing companion? That should be a designed path, not an abandonment.
6. **Is the caregiver a user or a customer or a patient?** InspireD improved outcomes for the person with dementia and **not** for carers; the iCST app improved carer EQ-5D and **not** patient outcomes. The evidence is genuinely inconsistent about who benefits, and we are asking the carer to do all the work.
7. **Can the "no wrong answers" principle and a spaced-repetition algorithm coexist?** SM-2/FSRS require a graded difficulty signal from the learner. If we never show failure, where does the signal come from — latency, caregiver rating, implicit hint-usage? This is an unresolved design contradiction at the heart of the concept.
8. **Unverified competitor claim to check before any competitive positioning:** Memory Lane Games' outcome percentages differ between their own site (67%/66%, caregiver survey) and third-party coverage describing "a randomized controlled trial" (92%). I could not locate a published trial. Do not repeat either figure without a primary source.
