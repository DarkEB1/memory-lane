# Does cognitive training / stimulation actually change dementia trajectory?

**Domain research for the personal-content memory-training app.**
Prepared 2026-08-12. All substantive claims carry author/year + title + URL.

---

## Summary (10 bullets)

1. **Nothing in this literature modifies disease.** There is no RCT evidence that cognitive stimulation, cognitive training, or cognitive rehabilitation slows Alzheimer's neuropathology, alters amyloid/tau biomarkers, or delays institutionalisation or death. The 2024 Lancet Commission update makes no disease-modification claim for any psychosocial intervention. Effects are **symptomatic and while-you-do-it**.
2. **Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (CST) is the strongest thing in the field, and it is small.** Cochrane 2023: 37 RCTs, 2,766 people, cognition SMD 0.40 (95% CI 0.25–0.55), ≈1.99 MMSE points, moderate certainty. The largest and most certain effect is not cognition at all — it is **communication and social interaction, SMD 0.53, high certainty**.
3. **Transfer barely exists.** Owen 2010 (n=11,430) found improvement on *every* trained task and **zero transfer even to closely related untrained tasks**. Simons et al. 2016 concluded there is "little evidence that training… improves everyday cognitive performance." Assume a card practised is a card learned, and nothing more.
4. **Cognitive rehabilitation proves the point brutally.** Cochrane 2023 (Kudlicka): huge effects on the *specific everyday goals trained* (SMD 1.46, high certainty) and **negligible or slightly negative** effects on memory, attention, general function, QoL and mood. Train the thing you want; do not expect spillover.
5. **The closest analogue to our app failed on cognition.** iCST (Orrell 2017, PLoS Med): caregiver-delivered, home-based, individual cognitive stimulation, 75 × 30-min sessions over 25 weeks. **No cognitive effect** (ADAS-Cog MD −0.55, p = 0.45). 22% of dyads did zero sessions. What *did* improve: how the person with dementia rated the relationship with their caregiver, and caregiver quality of life.
6. **Adherence, not algorithm, is the binding constraint.** Cochrane CST subgroup analysis: benefit is clearest at **≥2 sessions/week**; iCST showed only 40% of family dyads sustained that. Chan 2024: **supervised** computerised training beat unsupervised across every memory domain (unsupervised improved verbal memory only, SMD 0.21).
7. **Cognitive reserve is real but it is not what people think.** High reserve ≈ 47% lower relative risk of MCI/dementia (HR 0.53) — but it works by *tolerating* pathology, not reducing it, and once dementia is clinically evident, higher-reserve people **decline faster** (Wilson 2010: cognitive activity "compresses cognitive morbidity"). Reserve is a mid-life story. Our users are past that window.
8. **"Use it or lose it" at the synapse is a plausible mechanism, not a demonstrated one.** Aβ impairs hippocampal LTP and synapse loss is the strongest pathological correlate of cognitive decline — but animal enrichment studies contradict each other on whether activity touches amyloid at all (Lazarov 2005 vs Arendash 2004), and no human trial has shown it.
9. **Spaced retrieval genuinely works — narrowly.** Spaced retrieval training reliably teaches *specific* facts/associations to people with AD, and Middleton & Schwartz's critical review found effortful spaced retrieval **equals or beats errorless learning**, with more durable gains. This is real support for a spaced-repetition engine — for the items in the deck, and only those.
10. **There is a documented harm channel.** 16% of AD patients showed a **catastrophic reaction during neuropsychological testing** (Tiberti 1998). A daily app that repeatedly asks a person to name a face they can no longer name is a testing paradigm. Design must make failure non-events.

## Definitions — three distinct things, routinely blurred

These are separate interventions with separate evidence bases. Conflating them is the single most common error in this space (including in marketing by commercial "brain training" vendors).

| | **Cognitive stimulation (CS/CST)** | **Cognitive training (CT)** | **Cognitive rehabilitation (CR)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | Enjoyable, general activities engaging thinking/memory/concentration, usually in a small social group | Guided repeated practice on standardised tasks targeting specific domains (memory, attention, speed), graded difficulty | Individualised, collaborative goal-setting to manage *specific real-life activities*; compensation as much as restoration |
| Aim | Global engagement + wellbeing | Improve/maintain the trained cognitive function | Improve functioning in a named everyday task |
| Best evidence | Woods 2023 Cochrane, SMD 0.40 cognition | Bahar-Fuchs 2019 Cochrane, SMD 0.42 global cognition | Kudlicka 2023 Cochrane, SMD 1.46 goal attainment |
| Generalises? | Modestly, to communication/QoL | Barely — near transfer only | **No** — goal-specific by design and by result |

Sources:
- Woods B, Rai HK, Elliott E, Aguirre E, Orrell M, Spector A (2023). *Cognitive stimulation to improve cognitive functioning in people with dementia.* Cochrane Database Syst Rev, CD005562. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39804128/
- Bahar-Fuchs A, Martyr A, Goh AMY, Sabates J, Clare L (2019). *Cognitive training for people with mild to moderate dementia.* Cochrane Database Syst Rev, CD013069. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30909318/
- Kudlicka A, Martyr A, Bahar-Fuchs A, Sabates J, Woods B, Clare L (2023). *Cognitive rehabilitation for people with mild to moderate dementia.* Cochrane Database Syst Rev, CD013388. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37389428/

**Our app is a hybrid**: mechanically it is cognitive training (repeated graded retrieval practice); in content it is reminiscence/cognitive stimulation (personal photos, family names, life events); in intent it is closest to cognitive rehabilitation (help this person keep hold of *these specific people and facts that matter to them*). The CR framing is the most defensible, and it comes with CR's evidence — big effects on trained targets, none elsewhere.

## Key findings with citations

### A. Cognitive Stimulation Therapy — the best-evidenced psychosocial intervention in dementia

**Woods et al. 2023, Cochrane CD005562** (37 RCTs, n = 2,766, median age 79.7, mild-to-moderate dementia). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39804128/
- Cognition: **SMD 0.40 (95% CI 0.25–0.55)**, moderate-certainty evidence; on MMSE, **1.99 points (1.24–2.74)** across 25 studies / 1,893 participants.
- **Communication and social interaction: SMD 0.53 (0.36–0.70), high-certainty evidence** (5 studies, 702 participants). This is the review's strongest and most certain result.
- Self-rated quality of life: SMD 0.25 (0.07–0.42), moderate certainty.
- Slight benefits for depressed mood, anxiety, IADLs, and behaviour that challenges.
- Caregiver anxiety/depression: **little or no difference**.
- **Dose subgroup:** benefits clearest with **two or more sessions per week** vs once weekly.
- **Severity subgroup:** benefits clearest in **mild** dementia; smaller in moderate.
- No significant difference individual vs group delivery, or community vs care home.
- **Durability unknown:** only 8 of 37 studies reported any follow-up data. Cochrane's conclusion is explicitly framed as "small, **short-term** cognitive benefits."

**Spector et al. 2003, BJPsych** — the founding CST RCT (n = 201). MMSE p = 0.044, ADAS-Cog p = 0.014, QoL-AD p = 0.028; NNT = 6 for a ≥4-point ADAS-Cog improvement; authors state results "compare favourably with trials of drugs for dementia." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12948999/

**Orrell et al. 2014, BJPsych — Maintenance CST (MCST).** Continuing CST beyond the initial programme produced **no significant cognitive benefit overall at 6 months**. A cognition benefit appeared only in the subgroup taking acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (MMSE p = 0.03 at 3 and 6 months). Self-rated QoL improved at 6 months. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24676963/
→ *This matters a lot for a daily app: the "just keep doing it forever and gains keep accruing" model is not supported. Maintenance sustained wellbeing, not cognition.*

**2024 Lancet Commission update** (Sommerlad et al., *Alzheimers Dement* 2025;20(Suppl 4):e085068). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11713387/
- Supports CST (moderate quality, ~1.99 MMSE points) and tailored activity programmes (QoL Cohen's d 0.79; neuropsychiatric symptoms d 0.62; carer burden d 0.68).
- **Exercise interventions in people who already have dementia: "not effective in improving neuropsychiatric symptoms, cognition or functioning."**
- **No disease-modification claim is made for any of these.**

### B. Cognitive training in dementia — modest, low-confidence, no functional payoff

**Bahar-Fuchs et al. 2019, Cochrane CD013069** (33 RCTs, mild-moderate dementia). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30909318/
- Global cognition **SMD 0.42 (0.23–0.62)**, moderate certainty; verbal semantic fluency **SMD 0.52 (0.23–0.81)**, high certainty; gains persisted 3–12 months.
- **"Unable to determine whether CT was associated with any meaningful gains"** for mood, behaviour, ADLs, QoL — very low certainty.
- Compared against *active* alternatives (OT, mindfulness, reminiscence): **little to no effect on global cognition**. i.e. much of the benefit may be non-specific attention/engagement.
- Almost all trials at high or unclear risk of selection and performance bias (no blinding, weak allocation concealment).

**Chan ATC, Ip RTF, Tran JYS, Chan JYC, Tsoi KKF (2024). *Computerized cognitive training for memory functions in mild cognitive impairment or dementia: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* npj Digital Medicine.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38172429/ (35 RCTs)
- **MCI** (n = 1,489): verbal memory SMD 0.55 (0.35–0.74); visual memory 0.36 (0.12–0.60); working memory 0.37 (0.10–0.64).
- **Dementia** (n = 371): **only** verbal memory improved, SMD 0.64 (0.02–1.27) — CI almost touching zero, tiny sample.
- **Supervised CCT showed larger effects across domains; unsupervised CCT improved verbal memory only (SMD 0.21).**
→ *The unsupervised, at-home condition — which is exactly our product — has the weakest effect in the meta-analysis.*

**Gates NJ et al. 2019, Cochrane CD012279, *Computerised cognitive training for preventing dementia in people with mild cognitive impairment*.** https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD012279.pub2/full
- Only 8 trials met the ≥12-week criterion; small samples; very low quality evidence; high/unclear risk of bias especially blinding.
- **Data on incident dementia were missing across all trials.** There is therefore **no evidence at all** that computerised cognitive training prevents or delays dementia in MCI. Not weak evidence — absent evidence.

### C. Cognitive rehabilitation — large effects, and they do not travel

**Kudlicka et al. 2023, Cochrane CD013388** (6 RCTs, n = 1,702, mean age 76–80, 58.9% Alzheimer's). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37389428/
- Goal attainment at end of treatment: self-rated **SMD 1.46 (1.26–1.66)**, informant-rated **1.61 (1.01–2.21)**, satisfaction **1.31 (1.09–1.54)** — **high-certainty evidence**, sustained at 3–12 month follow-up.
- **Negligible effects** on anxiety, quality of life, sustained attention, memory, delayed recall, and general functional ability at end of treatment.
- At follow-up: **small negative effects** on general functional ability (moderate certainty), memory, and anxiety.

**Clare et al. 2019, GREAT trial, Int J Geriatr Psychiatry** (n = 475, 10 weekly sessions + 4 maintenance). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30724405/
- Goal attainment: d = 0.97 (participant) / 1.11 (informant) at 3 months; d = 0.94 / 0.96 at 9 months.
- **"There were no significant differences in secondary outcomes"** — quality of life, mood, self-efficacy, cognition, or study-partner stress/wellbeing, at either timepoint.

**Interpretation:** the largest, cleanest effects in the entire dementia psychosocial literature are goal-specific. Nothing generalises. This is the single most reliable finding in the domain and it should shape the product's promise.

### D. The closest thing to our app that has been trialled — and it did not work on cognition

**Orrell M, Yates L, Leung P, et al. (2017). *The impact of individual Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (iCST) on cognition, quality of life, caregiver health, and family relationships in dementia: A randomised controlled trial.* PLoS Medicine.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28350796/
- **Design:** family caregiver delivers 30-minute individual cognitive stimulation sessions at home; **75 sessions over 25 weeks** (3×/week target). Directly analogous to a caregiver-configured daily home app.
- **Cognition (ADAS-Cog, co-primary): no difference. MD −0.55, p = 0.45.**
- **Adherence collapsed:** only 40% of dyads managed ≥2 sessions/week; **22% completed no sessions at all**.
- **What improved:** people with dementia rated the **relationship with their caregiver** more positively; caregivers reported better quality of life (EQ-5D).
- Authors attribute the null cognitive result partly to low adherence.

**Design consequence:** the honest expected value of a home, caregiver-mediated, personal-content memory app is **relational and affective, not cognitive** — plus whatever specific items get genuinely learned via spaced retrieval. Build for that.

### E. Reminiscence therapy — relevant because our content *is* personal life material

**Woods B, O'Philbin L, Farrell EM, Spector AE, Orrell M (2018). *Reminiscence therapy for dementia.* Cochrane Database Syst Rev, CD001120** (22 studies, n = 1,972; meta-analysis 16 studies, n = 1,749). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30092689/
- Some evidence for improved quality of life, cognition, communication and possibly mood — **"all the benefits were small"** and inconsistent across modality and setting.
- Communication/interaction vs no-treatment control: **SMD −0.51 (−0.97 to −0.05)** (sign convention favours reminiscence).
- **Individual** reminiscence → cognition and mood; **group** reminiscence → communication.
- QoL benefits most apparent in care-home settings.

This supports personal-content-as-material on wellbeing/communication grounds. It does **not** support personal content as a cognitive-decline countermeasure.

### F. Transfer, and the collapse of the "brain training" claim

**Owen AM, Hampshire A, Grahn JA, et al. (2010). *Putting brain training to the test.* Nature 465:775–778.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20407435/
- 11,430 participants, 6 weeks of online training on reasoning, memory, planning, visuospatial, attention tasks.
- Verbatim: *"Although improvements were observed in every one of the cognitive tasks that were trained, no evidence was found for transfer effects to untrained tasks, even when those tasks were cognitively closely related."*

**Simons DJ, Boot WR, Charness N, Gathercole SE, Chabris CF, Hambrick DZ, Stine-Morrow EAL (2016). *Do "Brain-Training" Programs Work?* Psychological Science in the Public Interest 17(3):103–186.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27697851/
- Verbatim: *"little evidence that training enhances performance on distantly related tasks or that training improves everyday cognitive performance."*
- Gradient found: strong gains on the exact trained task → some gain on closely related tasks → minimal evidence of transfer to everyday activities.
- **No study in their review conformed to all best-practice criteria** for drawing clear conclusions about everyday benefit.
- Population: predominantly healthy adults — this is not a dementia finding, but it is the ceiling on the mechanism.

**ACTIVE trial — Rebok GW, Ball K, Guey LT, et al. (2014). *Ten-Year Effects of the ACTIVE Cognitive Training Trial on Cognition and Everyday Functioning in Older Adults.* J Am Geriatr Soc.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24417410/
- n = 2,832 **healthy, independently-living** older adults, mean baseline age 73.6.
- At 10 years: **reasoning and speed-of-processing training maintained effects on their targeted abilities; memory training effects were no longer maintained for memory performance.**
- **Self-reported** IADL difficulty was lower in all three arms: effect sizes 0.48 (memory), 0.38 (reasoning), 0.36 (speed). ~60% of trained vs ~50% of controls at/above baseline self-reported IADL at 10 years.
- **Caveat that is usually dropped in citation:** the headline functional benefit is on *self-reported* difficulty. Self-report in an unblinded trial is vulnerable to expectancy. And the arm most similar to our app — **memory training** — is precisely the arm whose targeted cognitive gains **did not last**.

### G. Multidomain prevention trials — different population, and small

**Ngandu T, Lehtisalo J, Solomon A, et al. (2015). *A 2 year multidomain intervention of diet, exercise, cognitive training, and vascular risk monitoring versus control to prevent cognitive decline in at-risk elderly people (FINGER): a randomised controlled trial.* Lancet 385:2255–2263.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25771249/
- Between-group difference in NTB total score change **per year: 0.022 (95% CI 0.002–0.042, p = 0.030)** — i.e. Cohen's d ≈ 0.13 over two years.
- Population: **at-risk but cognitively unimpaired** community-dwelling older adults, not MCI, not dementia.

**Baker LD, Espeland MA, et al. (2025). *Structured vs Self-Guided Multidomain Lifestyle Interventions for Global Cognitive Function: The US POINTER Randomized Clinical Trial.* JAMA.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40720610/
- n = 2,111, mean age 68.2, at risk for cognitive decline, **no dementia**.
- Structured vs self-guided: **0.029 SD per year (95% CI 0.008–0.050, p = .008)**.
- **Both arms improved** (structured 0.243 SD/yr; self-guided 0.213 SD/yr). There was **no no-intervention control**, so a large share of the within-arm improvement is likely practice/expectancy effects, not intervention.
- Fewer serious adverse events in the structured arm (151 vs 190).

**Honest reading:** these are the flagship multidomain prevention trials, and the incremental effect of the *more intensive* arm is ~0.03 SD/year. They are also **prevention trials in people without dementia** — they say nothing about whether an intervention helps someone who already has AD or MCI, and cognitive training was only one of four components.

### H. Cognitive reserve — supported, but not the story people tell

**Nelson ME, Jester DJ, Petkus AJ, Andel R (2021). *Cognitive Reserve, Alzheimer's Neuropathology, and Risk of Dementia: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.* Neuropsychol Rev.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7790730/
- High cognitive reserve ≈ **47% lower relative risk of MCI or dementia (pooled HR 0.53)**; 62% for residual-based measures, 48% for proxy measures (education, occupation).
- CR protects **"above and beyond the effect of AD-related structural pathology and biomarkers"** — i.e. it does **not** prevent or reduce accumulation of AD pathology. It buys clinical tolerance of pathology.

**Wilson RS, Barnes LL, Aggarwal NT, et al. (2010). *Cognitive activity and the cognitive morbidity of Alzheimer disease.* Neurology 75(11):990–996.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20811001/
- Frequent cognitively stimulating activity in old age predicted **slower decline before dementia onset and faster decline after it** — "compresses the cognitive morbidity associated with AD."
- This is *good* for lived experience and terrible for the naive claim "stimulation slows the disease." It does not slow the disease; it shifts when the impairment shows up.

**Wilson RS, Boyle PA, Yu L, Barnes LL, Schneider JA, Bennett DA (2013). *Life-span cognitive activity, neuropathologic burden, and cognitive aging.* Neurology.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23825173/
- More frequent lifespan cognitive activity associated with slower late-life decline **independent of** infarcts, Lewy bodies, amyloid burden and tangle density; accounted for 14% of residual variance in decline not explained by neuropathology.
- **Observational.** Reverse causation is a live threat: preclinical dementia reduces cognitive activity years before diagnosis. This cannot establish that adding activity in late life helps.

**Cognitive reserve after diagnosis — IDEAL study** (Age and Ageing 2025). https://academic.oup.com/ageing/article/54/1/afae284/7944082 — higher-reserve individuals showed steeper post-diagnosis decline but still better absolute cognitive function at 2 years.

**Bottom line on reserve:** it is a *lifecourse* construct built mainly in education, mid-life occupational complexity and long-run engagement. It is (b) *promising/supported observationally* as an explanation for who gets diagnosed when. It is **not** evidence that a 20-minute daily app started at age 78 with an existing diagnosis builds reserve.

### I. Synaptic plasticity and "use it or lose it" — plausible mechanism only

- Aβ disrupts hippocampal long-term potentiation, the cellular correlate of learning and memory, contributing to early cognitive deficits *before* neuronal loss. Skaper SD, Facci L, Zusso M, Giusti P (2017). *Synaptic Plasticity, Dementia and Alzheimer Disease.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28088900/ ; Ebrahimi R, Golzari Z, et al. (2025). *Impaired Synaptic Plasticity Mechanisms in Alzheimer's Disease.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41021123/
- **Animal enrichment evidence is contradictory:**
  - Lazarov O et al. (2005). *Environmental enrichment reduces Abeta levels and amyloid deposition in transgenic mice.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15766532/ — enrichment **reduced** amyloid.
  - Arendash GW et al. (2004). *Environmental enrichment improves cognition in aged Alzheimer's transgenic mice despite stable beta-amyloid deposition.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15257141/ — cognition improved with **amyloid unchanged**.
  - Costa DA et al. (2007). *Enrichment improves cognition in AD mice by amyloid-related and unrelated mechanisms.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16730391/
- **No human RCT** has demonstrated that cognitive intervention changes amyloid, tau, or brain atrophy. A PubMed search for RCTs of cognitive training with AD biomarker endpoints returns only protocols and secondary-outcome studies (e.g. Prakash RS et al., JMIR Res Protoc, PMID 41973869).

**Verdict:** the AD-plasticity story is **(c) plausible mechanism only**. "Use it or lose it" at the synapse is folk-model-adjacent: it borrows the language of a real neurobiological phenomenon (activity-dependent plasticity) to license a claim (that daily quizzing preserves synapses in a person with AD) that has never been tested in humans, and whose animal analogue is contradictory. **Do not put this in the product copy.**

### J. Learning mechanics — where the evidence is genuinely on our side

**Middleton EL, Schwartz MF (2012). *Errorless Learning in Cognitive Rehabilitation: A Critical Review.* Neuropsychological Rehabilitation 22(2):138–168.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3381647/
- The classic rationale for errorless learning: amnesic patients with impaired explicit memory cannot use conscious recollection to reject their own errors, so errors get reinforced implicitly (Hebbian).
- **But the review finds this overstated.** Retrieval-practice methods often match or exceed errorless learning in AD/dementia. Errorless and massed conditions produce few or no errors during training "yet these types of training were associated with the poorest performance on final measures." Errorless gains show more forgetting over longer intervals; **effortful methods produce more durable gains**.
- Conclusion: *"increasing retrieval difficulty and likelihood of retrieval errors during training produce the best long-term performance benefits"* — particularly when difficulty is optimised through **spacing**.

**Oren S, Willerton C, Small J (2014). *Effects of Spaced Retrieval Training on Semantic Memory in Alzheimer's Disease: A Systematic Review.* J Speech Lang Hear Res.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24023380/
- 12 high-quality studies; **"SRT was shown to have important positive effects on learning semantic information across the included studies."**
- Explicitly flagged gaps: **variability in protocols, effect of dementia severity, maintenance effects, and generalization** all remain unresolved.

**This is the honest engineering foundation for the app.** Spaced retrieval is (a) *proven* for acquiring and retaining specific target information in AD. Its generalisation and long-term maintenance are (b) *underpowered/unresolved*. That maps exactly onto what a spaced-repetition scheduler should promise: *these particular cards*, and no claim beyond them.

### K. Harms — specific, documented, and designable-around

- **Catastrophic reactions during cognitive testing.** Tiberti C, Sabe L, Kuzis G, et al. (1998). *Prevalence and correlates of the catastrophic reaction in Alzheimer's disease.* Neurology 50(2):546–548. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9484396/ — **16% of 146 AD patients showed a catastrophic reaction during neuropsychological evaluation.** Two components: an anxious/angry one (associated with irritability, longer disease duration) and a depressive one (associated with more severe cognitive impairment and older age).
  → *A daily retrieval-practice session is structurally a neuropsychological evaluation. Roughly one in six people with AD reacts catastrophically in that setting, and the risk rises with severity.*
- **Antecedents in real settings:** Yeom HA, Watson NM (2009). *Patterns of antecedents of catastrophic reactions in nursing home residents with dementia*, 229 CR events analysed. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25030468/
- **Awareness of deficit is bound up with wellbeing.** Cines S, Farrell M, Steffener J, et al. (2015). *Examining the Pathways Between Self-Awareness and Well-Being in Mild to Moderate Alzheimer Disease.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26560509/ ; Woods RT, Nelis SM, Martyr A, et al. (2014). *What contributes to a good quality of life in early dementia? Awareness and the QoL-AD.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24919416/ (final model accounted for 57% of variance in QoL-AD).
  → *An app that systematically surfaces "you used to know this and now you don't" is an awareness-of-deficit machine. That is the mechanism most plausibly linked to depression and reduced QoL in this population.*
- **Reality-orientation-style repeated correction** — particularly repeatedly informing someone of a bereavement they have forgotten — is widely held in clinical practice to cause fresh grief and distress in moderate-to-severe dementia, and is why validation-style approaches displaced rigid reality orientation. *Note: I could not retrieve a high-quality primary RCT quantifying this harm; treat as (c) clinical consensus / plausible mechanism, not (a) proven. It is nonetheless a design red line because the downside is catastrophic and the cost of avoiding it is zero.*
- **Reassuring counter-evidence on the intervention side:** Cochrane and trial-level Data Safety Monitoring Boards for cognitive training/rehabilitation reported no significant participant harms (see Bahar-Fuchs 2013/2019 and Clare/GREAT). The harm risk is not from "cognitive activity"; it is from *the way failure is staged*.

## Evidence quality assessment

| Claim | Grade | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| CST produces small short-term cognitive benefit in mild-moderate dementia | **(a) Proven** | Cochrane, 37 RCTs, moderate certainty, SMD 0.40. Ceiling ≈ 2 MMSE points. |
| CST improves communication and social interaction | **(a) Proven** | High-certainty GRADE, SMD 0.53 — the single most certain effect in the field |
| Cognitive training improves the trained function | **(a) Proven** | Cochrane SMD 0.42; Owen 2010 — trained tasks always improve |
| Cognitive rehabilitation improves the *specific goals trained* | **(a) Proven** | Cochrane high certainty, SMD 1.46; GREAT d ≈ 1.0 |
| Spaced retrieval teaches specific facts/associations in AD | **(a) Proven** (narrow) | Oren 2014, 12 studies |
| Effortful spaced retrieval ≥ errorless learning for durability | **(a/b) Well-supported** | Middleton & Schwartz 2012 critical review |
| Personal/reminiscence content improves QoL, mood, communication | **(b) Promising, small, inconsistent** | Woods 2018 Cochrane; benefits "all small" |
| Computerised training helps memory in MCI | **(b) Promising** | Chan 2024, SMD 0.36–0.55, high heterogeneity |
| **Unsupervised** home computerised training helps | **(b) Weak** | Chan 2024: verbal memory only, SMD 0.21 |
| Multidomain lifestyle programmes help cognitively-unimpaired at-risk adults | **(b) Real but tiny** | FINGER d ≈ 0.13 / 2 yr; POINTER 0.029 SD/yr incremental |
| Cognitive reserve reduces risk of clinical dementia | **(b) Strong observational, not causal** | HR 0.53; lifecourse construct; confounding + reverse causation unresolved |
| Cognitive activity in late life protects synapses / alters AD pathology | **(c) Mechanism only** | Human RCT evidence absent; animal data contradictory |
| Caregiver-delivered home cognitive stimulation improves cognition | **(d) Tested and negative** | iCST, n large, ADAS-Cog p = 0.45 |
| Brain training transfers to untrained tasks / everyday cognition | **(d) Tested and negative** | Owen 2010 (n=11,430); Simons 2016 |
| Cognitive training prevents or delays dementia in MCI | **(d) No evidence exists** | Gates 2019 Cochrane: **no trial reported incident dementia** |
| Any cognitive intervention modifies AD pathology or biomarkers | **(d) No evidence exists** | No RCT with biomarker endpoints has shown change |
| Maintenance CST sustains cognitive gains long-term | **(d) Tested, largely negative** | Orrell 2014: null overall; positive only in AChEI subgroup |

**Systemic weaknesses across this entire literature:** near-universal absence of participant/personnel blinding; heavy reliance on unblinded self-report for functional outcomes; small samples in dementia (vs MCI); publication bias; short follow-up (only 8/37 CST trials reported any); and comparison against passive rather than active controls — where active controls *are* used (Bahar-Fuchs 2019), the effect on global cognition largely disappears.

## DIRECT DESIGN IMPLICATIONS

### Claims and framing

1. **DO frame the product as: "keeps the people and stories that matter most present and practised, and gives you something warm to do together."** Every load-bearing word is defensible: retrieval practice works on the practised items (Oren 2014); reminiscence content improves communication/mood modestly (Woods 2018); caregiver-delivered stimulation improved *relationship quality and caregiver QoL* even when it failed on cognition (Orrell 2017).
2. **NEVER claim the app slows, delays, or halts dementia, Alzheimer's, or cognitive decline.** No evidence exists. Gates 2019 found zero trials even *measuring* dementia incidence; no trial has shown biomarker change.
3. **NEVER claim "builds cognitive reserve."** Reserve is a lifecourse construct built decades earlier, and it works by tolerating pathology, not preventing it (Nelson 2021). Worse, the reserve literature implies faster decline post-diagnosis (Wilson 2010) — the claim isn't just unsupported, it's backwards for our users.
4. **NEVER claim transfer** — no "improves your memory," "sharpens your mind," "better focus and recall." Owen 2010 and Simons 2016 are unambiguous. Say "helps hold on to *these* names and *these* stories."
5. **NEVER use "use it or lose it," "exercise for your brain," or neuroplasticity/synapse imagery.** (c)-grade mechanism dressed as (a)-grade fact. It also implies fault: if you lose it, you didn't use it enough.
6. **DO state the honest goal as maintenance and engagement, not improvement.** Every durable finding in this field is either goal-specific (CR) or wellbeing/communication (CST, reminiscence). Improvement in general cognition is not on the menu.
7. **DO give the doctor/researcher surface an explicit evidence statement** — a one-screen, cited summary of exactly what the app is and is not claiming. Clinicians will otherwise assume the worst (another brain-training product) and be right to.

### Content and mechanics

8. **DO make the deck the promise.** Cards are the unit of benefit. "Kept 42 of 45 family faces in active recall this month" is a defensible, measurable claim. "Improved cognition" is not.
9. **DO use spaced retrieval with real (spaced, effortful) retrieval attempts** rather than pure errorless presentation. Middleton & Schwartz: retrieval difficulty optimised by spacing produces the most durable gains, and errorless learning shows *more* forgetting.
10. **BUT cap difficulty asymmetrically.** The standard SM-2/FSRS objective — schedule at ~90% or ~85–90% retention — is tuned for a healthy learner where a lapse costs nothing emotionally. Here a lapse costs (potentially) a catastrophic reaction in ~1 in 6 people. **Target a much higher success rate (aim ≈95%+ correct-on-first-view) and let the algorithm trade efficiency for dignity.** Efficiency is worthless if the person stops using it or is distressed by it.
11. **DO use cueing hierarchies / vanishing cues rather than binary pass-fail.** A card should never be able to end in "wrong." It should degrade gracefully: free recall → first-letter/semantic cue → multiple choice → recognition → simple reminiscence prompt ("this is your daughter Anne — tell me about her"). Every path terminates in a success or in a warm, answerable prompt.
12. **NEVER show a "you got this wrong" state, a score, a streak-broken state, a percentage-correct, or a declining chart to the patient.** These are awareness-of-deficit surfaces, the mechanism most plausibly linked to depression and lower QoL (Cines 2015; Woods 2014). Longitudinal performance data goes to the clinician surface only, and to the caregiver only with deliberate framing.
13. **DO instrument for retirement of items.** When a card repeatedly fails even at the recognition level, it must **silently leave the rotation** and reappear only as reminiscence content. Never keep re-presenting a face that has been lost — that is a scheduled bereavement.
14. **NEVER auto-schedule content about deceased relatives without an explicit caregiver-set flag**, and never phrase a prompt in a way that requires the person to state or discover a death. The clinical consensus against confrontational reality orientation is not RCT-grade, but the asymmetry of harm makes this a free decision.
15. **DO let the caregiver mark content as sensitive/emotionally loaded** (estrangements, losses, trauma, a former home) with per-item suppression, and default to *excluding* anything not explicitly approved.

### Dose and session design

16. **DO target ~2–3 short sessions per week as the *floor*, not 7.** Cochrane's dose subgroup shows the clearest benefit at ≥2 sessions/week; iCST proved that a 3×/week caregiver-delivered target collapses in the real world (22% did nothing). A daily-streak design will manufacture guilt and dropout. **Design the reward around ≥2 sessions/week and treat anything more as bonus.**
17. **DO keep sessions short** — CST sessions are ~45 min *with a group facilitator*; iCST used 30 min and failed on adherence. For unsupervised home use, target 5–15 minutes.
18. **DO build in the supervision effect.** Chan 2024's clearest moderator is supervision: supervised CCT beat unsupervised across every domain. We cannot put a clinician in the room, but we can make the caregiver a *co-participant* rather than an administrator — sessions designed to be done *together*, with the relative's recorded voice as the cue. That is also the arm of iCST that actually produced benefit.
19. **DO stratify by severity, and say so.** CST benefit was clearest in mild dementia and smaller in moderate; Chan 2024 found meaningful effects in MCI and only one fragile domain in dementia. **The pilot's strongest case is MCI and mild dementia.** In moderate dementia, reposition the app explicitly toward reminiscence/connection and drop retrieval-practice framing entirely.
20. **DO NOT promise that stopping is safe or that gains persist.** Only 8/37 CST trials had follow-up; maintenance CST did not sustain cognitive benefit overall (Orrell 2014). Be honest with families: benefits are contemporaneous with use.

### Measurement (the doctor/researcher surface)

21. **DO measure what the evidence says actually moves:** goal attainment on caregiver-nominated targets (the CR model — highest, most certain effect sizes in the field), communication/social interaction, self-rated QoL (QoL-AD), caregiver QoL and relationship quality, and per-item retention. **Do not make MMSE/ADAS-Cog the headline metric** — we would be picking the field's weakest and most contested endpoint.
22. **DO log an in-session distress/abort signal** (caregiver-reported or inferred from abandonment) as a first-class safety outcome. Given Tiberti's 16%, this is the adverse-event measure a pilot needs and none of the commercial products collect.
23. **DO treat any within-person improvement as suspect.** POINTER's both-arms-improved result and ACTIVE's self-report caveat show how easily practice effects and expectancy masquerade as benefit. Any efficacy claim from the pilot needs an active control, not a waitlist.

## Open questions

1. **Does personally-meaningful content actually retain better than neutral content in AD?** Intuitively yes, and it underpins the whole product thesis — but the self-reference literature is thin here: Leblond et al. (2016) found mild-AD performance too low to analyse and only a partial, valence-dependent self-reference effect in aMCI (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26691733/). **This is a testable pilot hypothesis and arguably the most important one we own.**
2. **What is the right retention target for a dementia-adapted scheduler?** No published dose-response or desirable-difficulty curve exists for this population. Oren 2014 explicitly names protocol variability and severity effects as open. We will be picking a number (I'd argue ≈95%) on judgement, not evidence.
3. **Does the retrieval-practice benefit survive item loss as disease progresses, and what is the right retirement rule?** No literature on graceful item deprecation.
4. **Can an app substitute for the supervision effect?** Chan 2024 says supervision is the strongest moderator. Whether a caregiver-as-co-participant design recovers it is unknown and is a core pilot question.
5. **Does the relationship-quality benefit of iCST replicate when the delivery burden is shifted from the caregiver to software?** iCST's benefit came *with* an adherence failure; a lower-burden version might get more of both — or might lose the benefit if the benefit *was* the shared effort.
6. **Is there a distress signature we can detect passively?** Latency, abandonment, repeated skips. Unstudied; would be a genuine contribution.
7. **Does high engagement in a person with mild dementia predict the "compressed morbidity" pattern (Wilson 2010) — i.e. better now, steeper later?** If so, our long-run outcome graphs may look *worse* than controls after conversion, and we need to have said so in advance.
8. **Ethical:** if the honest claim is "this will not change the disease," will families still adopt it — and is there an obligation to make sure they are not buying hope? The pilot's consent materials should say plainly what §Claims 2–5 above forbid us from marketing.

## Two-sentence answer to the assigned question

**The strongest honest claim:** *"This app uses spaced retrieval — a technique with good evidence for helping people with Alzheimer's learn and hold on to specific information — applied to the faces, names and stories that matter most to this person; and it creates a structured, positive shared activity with family, which is the outcome that home-based cognitive stimulation has actually been shown to improve."*

**The unsupportable and unethical claims:** that it slows, delays or prevents dementia or Alzheimer's; that it builds or protects cognitive reserve; that it preserves synapses or brain tissue; that it improves memory, focus or thinking in general; that benefits transfer beyond the practised material; that it substitutes for or augments medication; or any outcome graph shown to a family that implies the disease's course has been altered.
