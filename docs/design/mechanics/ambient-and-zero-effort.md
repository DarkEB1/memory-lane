# Ambient & Zero-Effort Mechanics

**Lens:** The ambient/calm-technology designer.

Mechanics here demand little or no deliberate effort, no navigation, and often no
awareness that "an app" exists at all. They are designed for the patient who will
never open anything, for late-day fatigue, for advanced impairment, and for the
moments when a structured session has already been abandoned.

Governing principles for this lens:

- **The device moves first.** Nothing waits behind a tap the patient will never make.
- **Every mechanic must be valuable at zero response.** If the patient just watches,
  or walks away, the mechanic still did its job.
- **No mechanic may ever look like it is waiting to catch someone out.** Silence is
  an acceptable answer. Walking away is an acceptable answer.
- **Exposure counts.** Familiarity, priming and emotional contact are outcomes, not
  consolation prizes for failing the "real" exercise.

Divergent phase — nothing culled for feasibility.

---

## 1. The Frame That Waits

**One line:** A photo frame on the sideboard cycles through their life, and once in a
while it says one warm sentence out loud — "That's Margaret, on the boat at Whitby" —
and then goes quietly back to the pictures.

**Input needed:** 20+ photos with a one-line spoken or typed caption each (who, where,
roughly when). Captions can be typed and read by TTS, or recorded in a relative's voice.

**Interaction:** None required. Just watch. Optional: touch the screen to hear the
caption again.

**Why it might work:** Repeated errorless exposure to face–name–place bindings without
any retrieval demand. Spaced re-presentation across the day exploits the spacing effect
even in the absence of conscious effort. Statements rather than questions mean the
correct association is always the last thing heard, which is the core of errorless
learning. Emotional salience of personal photos drives attention where generic content
would be tuned out.

**Failure mode:** A photo of someone who has died, surfacing without warning, can
trigger fresh grief — repeatedly, because the loss is re-experienced as new each time.
A voice speaking unexpectedly in an empty room can be frightening or interpreted as an
intruder. Captions that are wrong (family got the year or the beach wrong) can be
distressing when the patient knows better and cannot correct it.

**Difficulty:** low
**Variant of:** novel

## 2. The Frame That Asks

**One line:** The same quiet frame, but occasionally the picture holds still and a
gentle voice asks one question — "Who's that with you?" — and then, whatever happens,
tells them the answer warmly and moves on.

**Input needed:** Same as The Frame That Waits, plus for each question-eligible photo:
the name of one person in it, and a fallback sentence for the device to say if nothing
is offered ("That's your brother Tom. He always did wear that hat.").

**Interaction:** Speak aloud, or say nothing. Both are complete responses. No screen
touch anywhere.

**Why it might work:** Free autobiographical retrieval with a guaranteed non-punishing
resolution. The generation attempt itself, even when unsuccessful, produces a
testing-effect benefit when followed immediately by the answer. Because the device
resolves every question itself, the patient can never be left in an unresolved
"I should know this" state.

**Failure mode:** The pause after the question is the danger zone — any silence longer
than a few seconds reads as expectant judgement. If the device answers too fast it feels
like being cut off and talked over; too slow and it feels like a test. Patients who are
aware they are being assessed may start avoiding the room the frame is in.

**Difficulty:** medium
**Variant of:** The Frame That Waits

## 3. Good Morning, It Is You

**One line:** At the same time each morning the tablet lights up on its own and a
familiar voice says good morning, tells them what day it is, where they are, and who is
coming today — then shows three pictures of those people and stops.

**Input needed:** A 10–20 second recorded greeting from one relative; the patient's
preferred name; the address or place name they consider home; a photo per regular
visitor; the visit schedule (or a weekly rota entered once).

**Interaction:** No input at all. Watch and listen.

**Why it might work:** Orientation support (time, place, person) is the single most
requested daily need and is normally delivered by a stressed carer answering the same
question repeatedly. Delivering it unprompted removes the humiliation of having to ask.
The fixed time builds an anchor that procedural/habit memory can attach to even when
episodic memory cannot. Priming the day's visitors before they arrive raises the chance
of recognition at the door.

**Failure mode:** Reads as institutional if the tone is announcement-like rather than
personal. Telling someone what day it is can foreground exactly the deficit they were
comfortably unaware of. If the schedule is wrong — a promised visitor doesn't come —
the device has manufactured disappointment. Waking someone who was still asleep is
worse than nothing.

**Difficulty:** low
**Variant of:** novel

## 4. One Thing On The Whole Screen

**One line:** The tablet shows exactly one large photograph and nothing else — no
buttons, no menus, no edges, no back arrow — and touching it anywhere plays a voice
telling them who it is.

**Input needed:** Photos with one recorded voice line each (relative's own voice
strongly preferred over TTS).

**Interaction:** Touch anywhere on the screen. There is no wrong place to touch.

**Why it might work:** Removes the entire class of failures caused by target
acquisition — no hitting the wrong control, no discovering a hidden gesture, no
recovering from a state you didn't mean to enter. Tremor and arthritis stop mattering
when the target is 100% of the surface. Because there is no navigation, there is no
"lost" state to be embarrassed about.

**Failure mode:** Some patients will never realise the screen is touchable at all, and
the mechanic silently becomes a static picture. Others will touch repeatedly and get the
same line each time, which can feel like being stuck. Accidental palm or sleeve contact
retriggers audio at random, which is confusing.

**Difficulty:** low
**Variant of:** novel

## 5. Two Doors

**One line:** Two large photographs, side by side, filling the screen — a voice asks
"Which one is your Alice?" and touching either one is met with warmth.

**Input needed:** 2+ photos of the target person; a pool of clearly-distinct foil
photos (different sex/age/era to keep difficulty genuinely low); the target's name as
the patient uses it ("your Alice", not "Alice Hunter").

**Interaction:** Tap one of two large pictures. Each target is half the screen.

**Why it might work:** Recognition rather than recall, which is preserved far longer.
Forced-choice with a 50% floor means there is almost always a success experience
available. Two targets is the maximum that can be held in a compromised working memory
alongside the question itself. Adjustable foil distance gives a difficulty dial that
never has to be shown to the patient.

**Failure mode:** Getting it wrong on a two-choice question about your own daughter is
uniquely wounding — the odds make the failure feel starker. Foils that resemble other
real relatives create genuine ambiguity where the "wrong" answer is arguably right.
Patients who detect the pattern know they are being tested.

**Difficulty:** low
**Variant of:** The Frame That Asks

## 6. Pure Exposure

**One line:** Faces and names drift slowly across the screen all afternoon and the
patient is never asked anything at all, ever.

**Input needed:** Nothing beyond existing photo and name content already in the app.
Derived entirely from what the family has already added.

**Interaction:** No input at all. Not even optional.

**Why it might work:** Targets familiarity and perceptual fluency rather than
declarative memory — mere exposure raises the felt "warmth" of a face even when the
name is unrecoverable, which is the mechanism behind a relative being greeted happily
without being named. Repeated pairing of face and printed name may support residual
implicit learning. Crucially, zero demand means zero possibility of failure, so this is
the mechanic that still works on the worst days.

**Failure mode:** Invisible value — families and care staff may see "nothing is
happening" and turn it off. Endless drifting content can become wallpaper the patient
stops seeing. Risks being experienced as a screensaver of dead people if the content mix
is not curated for tone.

**Difficulty:** low
**Variant of:** The Frame That Waits

## 7. The Session That Starts Itself

**One line:** Every day at four o'clock, without anyone doing anything, the tablet
chimes softly and the first picture is already there.

**Input needed:** A preferred time of day set once by the family (ideally chosen to
match the patient's best hours); the ordinary session content.

**Interaction:** No input to begin. Whatever the session itself asks for afterwards.

**Why it might work:** Eliminates initiation, which is often the true blocker — apathy
and executive dysfunction mean a patient who would happily do a session will never be
the one to start it. A fixed daily time recruits procedural and habit memory, the same
system that keeps someone reaching for the teapot at the same hour. It also relieves the
carer of being the one who "makes" them do it, which protects the relationship.

**Failure mode:** Starting mid-conversation, mid-meal or during a visitor is intrusive
and can read as the device demanding attention. A fixed time collides with fluctuating
good and bad days. Repeated ignored starts train the patient to ignore the device
entirely.

**Difficulty:** low
**Variant of:** novel

## 8. Fade To Rest

**One line:** If they stop answering, the questions quietly stop too — the pictures keep
going, then the music softens, then the screen dims to their favourite photo and nothing
more is ever asked.

**Input needed:** Nothing — derived. Optionally one nominated "resting photo" the family
chooses as the image the device settles on.

**Interaction:** No input at all; the mechanic is defined by the absence of input.

**Why it might work:** Guarantees that walking away, falling asleep or losing the thread
is never punished with a nagging device or an accusatory "are you still there?". Removes
the possibility of ending a session in failure, so the emotional residue of every session
is calm rather than defeat — which is what determines whether they engage tomorrow. Also
protects the outcome data from being polluted by non-responses recorded as errors.

**Failure mode:** A patient who was merely slow — thinking, or physically unable to reach
— experiences the device giving up on them, which is its own small humiliation. Care
staff may read the degraded state as a fault. If the fade is too fast, the device seems
uninterested.

**Difficulty:** medium
**Variant of:** novel

## 9. The Ringing Telephone

**One line:** A telephone on the side table rings the way telephones used to ring; they
pick up the handset and it is their son, saying hello and asking them one small question
about the picture on the frame beside it.

**Input needed:** Recorded call-shaped audio from a relative: a greeting, one question, a
pause, a warm answer, a goodbye. 45–90 seconds. Ideally several so it is not identical
each time.

**Interaction:** Lift a handset. Speak into it. Put it down.

**Why it might work:** Rides on deeply-embedded procedural memory for the telephone —
lifting a handset when it rings survives long after tablet affordances are lost, because
it was practised for sixty years. Social presence of a real, loved voice drives
engagement no interface can. The telephone frame also legitimises the patient talking
aloud to a device without feeling foolish.

**Failure mode:** The belief that the son is really on the line, followed by the
discovery that he is not, is a serious deception harm — and the discovery may recur
daily. Grief risk is severe if the recorded voice belongs to someone who has died.
Some patients find an unexpected ringing phone alarming, especially at night.

**Difficulty:** high
**Variant of:** novel

## 10. Message Waiting

**One line:** A small box by the chair has a slowly blinking light and one big button;
pressing it plays a short message a grandchild left for them, and the light goes out.

**Input needed:** One 15–40 second voice message per day or per week from any family
member, recorded on their phone. Optionally ending with a question they will answer next
visit.

**Interaction:** Press one large physical button.

**Why it might work:** Answering-machine procedural memory plus the powerful motivation
of an unheard message addressed personally to you. The blinking light is a passive,
patient prompt that never nags and creates no time pressure — it will still be blinking
in three hours. Anticipation and social reward do the motivational work that gamification
cannot. Unlike the ringing telephone, it makes no claim to be live, so there is no
deception.

**Failure mode:** A light that blinks for days because no one recorded anything is a
visible measure of family neglect. Repeatedly pressing the button after the message is
consumed and getting nothing feels like a broken promise. If the patient cannot recall
having heard it, the message is "new" every time — which may be fine, or may mean they
never move on.

**Difficulty:** medium
**Variant of:** The Ringing Telephone

## 11. The Living Room Channel

**One line:** Between the programmes, the television itself shows their own photographs
and plays their own songs, as if it were just another thing on the telly.

**Input needed:** Photos and music already in the app; a casting device on the TV; the
family's choice of which hours it may take over.

**Interaction:** No input at all. Watching television is the existing behaviour.

**Why it might work:** Meets the patient inside a lifelong, fully-preserved habit — the
television is the one screen they have never had to learn. No new device, no new posture,
no new affordance. Content arrives during already-passive attention, so there is no
switching cost. In care homes it reaches communal space where a personal tablet never
goes.

**Failure mode:** Hijacking the TV interrupts something the patient actually wanted to
watch, which is a real loss of autonomy over the one medium they still control.
Confusion about why "their pictures are on the telly" can be genuinely disorienting.
In a shared lounge, one resident's private life is broadcast to everyone.

**Difficulty:** medium
**Variant of:** The Frame That Waits

## 12. The Idle Screen Life

**One line:** Whenever the tablet is just sitting there doing nothing, it shows one
photograph and one short sentence about it — so the "off" state of the device is already
the content.

**Input needed:** Nothing — derived from existing photos and captions.

**Interaction:** No input at all. Optional touch to hear the sentence read aloud.

**Why it might work:** Captures the largest block of available time — the hours a device
spends idle — at zero cost of attention or initiation. Repeated glancing exposure across
the day beats a single concentrated session for building familiarity. Because it is the
idle state rather than an app, there is nothing to open, close, or get lost inside.

**Failure mode:** Contributes to the device becoming visual furniture. Text captions
fail entirely for low vision or lost reading ability, leaving an uncaptioned photo the
patient cannot place. On a shared care-home tablet, the idle screen shows the wrong
resident's life to whoever picks it up.

**Difficulty:** low
**Variant of:** The Frame That Waits

## 13. The Room Knows You Came In

**One line:** When they walk into the room, the tablet notices and says hello by name,
then shows a picture of whoever they saw most recently.

**Input needed:** The patient's preferred name recorded in a familiar voice; a small
pool of greeting lines so it does not repeat identically; presence detection hardware
(PIR sensor or on-device camera used only for presence).

**Interaction:** No input at all — walking into the room is the whole interaction.

**Why it might work:** Perfect timing without any initiation demand: content arrives at
the exact moment attention is available and the patient is already oriented toward the
device. Being greeted by name is a dignity intervention as much as a memory one — it
asserts that this room, and this thing, knows who you are. Contextual re-entry cues can
support the "why did I come in here" moment.

**Failure mode:** Serious privacy problem in a bedroom or a shared home, and camera-based
detection may be unacceptable to families or regulators regardless of how it is
implemented. Greeting the wrong person (a carer, a grandchild) by the patient's name is
absurd and undermines trust in everything else the device says. A device that responds to
movement can be interpreted as watching them.

**Difficulty:** high
**Variant of:** The Session That Starts Itself

## 14. The Weekly Card

**One line:** A card arrives in the post each week with four of their photographs and
four sentences printed large, and they can keep it in their pocket, put it on the
mantelpiece, or show it to a visitor.

**Input needed:** Nothing beyond existing content; a postal address; family opt-in to
printing costs. Optionally a handwritten line added by a relative before it prints.

**Interaction:** No input at all. Hold, look, carry, show to someone.

**Why it might work:** Paper needs no charging, no learning, no permission and no
posture. It survives being dropped and is legible in any light. It is inherently social
— a card can be handed to a visitor, which converts a solo memory exercise into
conversation. It also extends the app's content into the hours and rooms the device
cannot reach, and gives the family a visible artefact of their contribution.

**Failure mode:** Cards accumulate and the same faces recur, so a well-meaning object
becomes clutter someone eventually throws away — in front of the patient. Print cannot
be corrected after an error. A card that arrives addressed to them but that they cannot
interpret is a private failure with no device to soften it.

**Difficulty:** medium
**Variant of:** novel

## 15. The Book That Knows Its Page

**One line:** They turn the pages of an ordinary photo album on their lap, and as each
page opens a voice in the room quietly tells them who is on it.

**Input needed:** A printed photo book generated from app content, with an NFC tag or
printed marker per page; the same per-photo voice captions used elsewhere; a tablet or
speaker in the room to do the talking.

**Interaction:** Turn a page. That is all. No screen involved.

**Why it might work:** The photo album is possibly the most preserved artefact in this
population's procedural and social repertoire — turning pages requires no instruction at
any stage of impairment. The narration supplies the names the patient can no longer
retrieve, without them having to ask anyone, which preserves the dignity of "looking at
my photos" rather than "being helped". Physical page-turning also gives motor engagement
and a clear sense of progress and completion that a slideshow lacks.

**Failure mode:** The disembodied voice may be attributed to a person in the room who
isn't there. If the tag reading is unreliable the narration desynchronises from the page,
which is worse than silence — being told the wrong name for a face you are looking at is
actively corrosive. Reprinting the book when content changes is slow and costly.

**Difficulty:** high
**Variant of:** The Frame That Waits

## 16. The Keepsake That Speaks

**One line:** A smooth wooden pebble sits by the chair; picking it up and holding it
plays thirty seconds of their wife singing, and putting it down stops it.

**Input needed:** One or more 20–40 second audio clips of very high emotional salience
(a voice, a song they sang, a grandchild laughing); a physical object chosen by the
family, ideally something already meaningful.

**Interaction:** Pick up and hold an object. No screen, no button, no target.

**Why it might work:** Tangible, warm, weighted objects invite handling from people who
will not touch a screen, and handling is self-soothing in agitation. Emotional salience
of the audio bypasses declarative memory entirely — the response is affective and
immediate. Because the object looks like an ornament rather than technology, it carries
none of the "I can't work these things" threat that a tablet does.

**Failure mode:** An object that talks when picked up can be genuinely frightening or
read as a hallucination, particularly at night or in delirium. It may be pocketed, hidden,
given away or washed. Highly salient audio of a deceased spouse can trigger acute grief
with no carer present to contain it.

**Difficulty:** high
**Variant of:** Message Waiting

## 17. Their Own Radio Station

**One line:** A wireless on the shelf plays the music of their twenties all day, and
every so often between the songs a familiar voice says a short thing about their life,
like a station announcer who happens to know them.

**Input needed:** Era and taste information (birth year, three favourite artists); 6+
short recorded "announcements" from family (30 seconds each); optionally licensed period
music or the family's own playlist.

**Interaction:** No input at all. Optionally one large dial for volume.

**Why it might work:** Music from ages 15–25 is the most durably encoded material most
people carry, and it primes autobiographical retrieval from the same period — so a
family announcement landing right after a period song hits an already-activated network.
Radio is a fully preserved format requiring no interaction. Continuous audio also fills
the silence that drives agitation and sundowning without demanding that anyone look at
anything.

**Failure mode:** Family voices spliced into a radio stream can be interpreted as the
relative being on the radio, or as voices in the house. Continuous audio in a shared care
home is antisocial and will be turned off by staff. A song attached to a bad memory can
land with no warning and no way to explain the sudden distress.

**Difficulty:** medium
**Variant of:** The Living Room Channel

## 18. Wind-Down

**One line:** As the light goes in the afternoon the tablet stops asking anything at all,
dims warm, and plays slow familiar photographs to slow familiar music until bedtime.

**Input needed:** A calm-content flag on photos the family judges soothing and
unambiguous; 20+ minutes of preferred calm music; the household's usual sundown hour.

**Interaction:** No input at all.

**Why it might work:** Directly targets sundowning — the late-day period where cognitive
demand backfires and agitation rises. Removing all demand at the exact hour capability
drops means the device stops being a source of stress precisely when it would otherwise
become one. Familiar music has strong evidence for agitation reduction. Predictable
evening ritual supports the sleep–wake cycle that further protects daytime cognition.

**Failure mode:** Signals "the day is over" to a patient who was still engaged, which is
patronising. Anything triggering on light level misfires in winter or in a dark room.
Slow, dim, wordless content can read as the device being broken or switched off.

**Difficulty:** medium
**Variant of:** Fade To Rest

## 19. The Question With No Wrong Answer

**One line:** A voice asks "Where did you and Joan go on holiday?" and then, whatever
they say — a right answer, a wrong answer, a story about something else entirely, or
nothing — it says "Whitby. Every August," and sounds delighted about it.

**Input needed:** Per item: a spoken question, the answer sentence in a warm human voice,
and one photograph. No answer-checking data required — the mechanic deliberately does not
grade.

**Interaction:** Speak aloud, or say nothing. Nothing is scored, nothing is judged.

**Why it might work:** Pure errorless learning delivered ambiently — the correct
association is always the last thing in the room, and an incorrect self-generated answer
is never reinforced by being marked wrong. The retrieval attempt still confers benefit.
Removing all judgement removes the anticipatory anxiety that stops many patients engaging
at all, and any offered story is treated as a contribution rather than an error, which
is the opposite of the tested-and-caught-out experience.

**Failure mode:** Patients with preserved insight can find unconditional delight
patronising and detect that they are being humoured. Because nothing is graded, the
researcher gets no performance signal from this mechanic — value must be measured
elsewhere. Answering "Whitby" for someone who now firmly believes it was Scarborough
creates a contradiction they cannot resolve.

**Difficulty:** medium
**Variant of:** The Frame That Asks

## 20. Just The One Word

**One line:** In the hallway, a screen shows one enormous word or short line all day —
"Today is Tuesday. Susan comes at 3." — and it changes only when the fact changes.

**Input needed:** The visit rota, or one line per day written by the family; the
patient's preferred names for people.

**Interaction:** No input at all. It is read, not used.

**Why it might work:** Answers the most-repeated question in dementia care without anyone
having to be asked it, which is a substantial dignity gain and a substantial carer relief.
Extremely low perceptual load — one line at very large size survives poor vision,
inattention and fatigue. Because it is static rather than cycling, it can be checked
repeatedly, and checking is never met with a change that invalidates the previous look.

**Failure mode:** Becomes wallpaper within a week and stops being seen. Wrong or stale
information is worse than none — a promised visitor who does not appear is a printed
disappointment. For patients who have lost reading, it is a permanent, silent reminder
that they used to read.

**Difficulty:** low
**Variant of:** Good Morning, It Is You

## 21. Someone In The Next Room

**One line:** A short silent loop plays on the screen of their daughter pottering in her
own kitchen, not looking at the camera, not saying anything to them — just present,
the way someone is present in the next room.

**Input needed:** 30–90 seconds of ambient video per relative in which the person is
visible but not addressing the camera; ideally in a domestic setting; no speech directed
at the patient.

**Interaction:** No input at all.

**Why it might work:** Targets social presence and loneliness rather than memory
performance — the felt sense of not being alone in the house, which is a large part of
what daily engagement is actually for. Non-addressing video makes no demand for a
response, so it cannot be failed and does not create the awkwardness of a video call.
Sustained non-demanding exposure to a face supports familiarity for that person's current
appearance, which often drifts away from the remembered version.

**Failure mode:** Can be understood as the relative actually being here, followed by the
distress of them being gone. Watching family live their lives without you can deepen
loneliness rather than relieve it. Uncanny on a loop — the same gesture recurring every
minute is unsettling once noticed.

**Difficulty:** medium
**Variant of:** Pure Exposure

## 22. Somebody Sat Down

**One line:** On the shared tablet in the care home lounge, a photograph is simply lying
there face-up like a magazine on the table, and whoever sits down can put a finger on it.

**Input needed:** Content flagged by the family as shareable in a communal space; a
lightweight way to identify who is sitting there (a chair tag, a staff tap, or content
neutral enough that it does not matter).

**Interaction:** Touch a photograph lying flat on a table. No login, no start, no
selection.

**Why it might work:** Reframes the exercise as a communal object rather than a personal
device, which lowers the threshold to engagement enormously in group settings — people
approach a thing on a table that they will not approach as "your therapy". A shared
surface makes reminiscence social, and residents narrating photos to each other does more
than any solo session. Zero-state design means there is nothing to be in the middle of
when someone walks away.

**Failure mode:** Privacy is the core problem — a shared surface can expose one
resident's family, home and history to everyone in the lounge, including people who will
misuse it. Wrong-person content produces genuine confusion about whose life is on the
table. Objects on shared tables get spilled on, taken, and fought over.

**Difficulty:** high
**Variant of:** One Thing On The Whole Screen

## 23. The Slow Reveal

**One line:** A photograph fades in over a full minute, out of focus at first and then
gradually sharper, and at any point they can say who it is — or not, and it simply
becomes clear on its own.

**Input needed:** Nothing — derived from existing photos. Optionally the family marks
which photos have a single clear subject.

**Interaction:** Speak aloud at any moment, or say nothing at all and just watch.

**Why it might work:** Creates a graded retrieval cue that self-adjusts to whatever the
patient's current capability is — an early recognition is a strong success, a late one is
still a success, and no recognition still ends in a clear image and a spoken name. This is
adaptive difficulty with no assessment, no levels and nothing for the patient to notice.
The slow onset also holds attention in a way a static image does not, and the resolution
is guaranteed, so it always terminates in clarity rather than in doubt.

**Failure mode:** Blur reads as broken eyesight to someone with cataracts or macular
degeneration, causing alarm about their vision rather than curiosity about the photo. The
slow reveal creates a suspense structure that some will experience as an unmistakable
test with a countdown. A minute is a long time for fluctuating attention.

**Difficulty:** medium
**Variant of:** The Frame That Asks

---

## 24. The Thing You Were Already Doing

**One line:** When they pick up the teapot in the morning, a photo of the kitchen they
had in 1968 appears on the counter screen — no question, no chime, just their own kitchen,
then and now.

**Input needed:** Photos tagged to an everyday activity or place (kitchen, chair, garden,
bedside); a sensor or smart-plug event tied to a real routine action; ideally 3+ photos
per routine so it is not identical daily.

**Interaction:** No input at all — performing an existing daily habit is the trigger.

**Why it might work:** Attaches memory content to preserved procedural routines, which
are the most reliably intact behaviours remaining. Because the trigger is something they
were going to do anyway, engagement requires zero initiation, zero motivation and zero
awareness of the system. Contextual match between the current physical setting and the
photographed setting is a powerful encoding-specificity cue for autobiographical
retrieval.

**Failure mode:** Requires household instrumentation most families will not have and care
homes will not permit. A screen lighting up unexpectedly during a kitchen task is a fall
risk if it startles. Content that appears without being asked for, in the middle of
something, may be experienced as the house doing things by itself — which for someone with
paranoid features is genuinely frightening.

**Difficulty:** high
**Variant of:** The Room Knows You Came In
