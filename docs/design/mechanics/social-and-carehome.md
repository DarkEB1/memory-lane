# Mechanics — Social & Care-Home Lens

**Lens:** The care-home activities coordinator. Every mechanic here has a second person in it — another resident, a staff member, a visiting grandchild, a family member 300 miles away. The single-player session is treated as the exception, not the default.

**Phase:** Divergent. Nothing culled for feasibility, cost, or weirdness.

**Framing constant across all of these:** co-op not competitive, contribution not testing, "tell them about it" not "do you remember it". The resident is a person with a life to show, not a subject being assessed.

---

## 1. Two Chairs, One Photo

- **oneLine:** You and the person sitting opposite you look at the same picture on a tablet lying flat between you, and each of you has a big button on your own side to say "I know this one".
- **inputNeeded:** Nothing new — derived from existing photo content of both residents; requires both residents enrolled and their families to have opted into shared viewing.
- **interaction:** Tap one large button on your own edge of a flat, table-mounted screen. Talking is expected but not required.
- **whyItMightWork:** Social facilitation and co-presence — recognition is easier and far less threatening when someone is beside you rather than assessing you. Neither person "loses"; both buttons light the same picture. Turn-taking is deeply preserved procedural social behaviour.
- **failureMode:** One resident is markedly more able and answers first every time, so the slower one withdraws and feels exposed in front of a peer. Also: seating a resident with someone they dislike turns the whole session sour.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** novel

## 2. Same Town, Same Time

- **oneLine:** A picture of a street, a factory or a beach comes up, and the tablet quietly tells you that the lady across the table lived a few streets away from there.
- **inputNeeded:** Place names and rough decades attached to each resident's photos/life events (family supplies place + year, even approximate); a small stock library of period street/landmark imagery to fill gaps.
- **interaction:** Just watch and talk; optional single tap on "that was my street".
- **whyItMightWork:** Autobiographical retrieval via shared context cueing — a co-witness to the same era and geography is the strongest possible retrieval cue, and the conversation carries the recall rather than the app. Builds durable peer relationships, which is the coordinator's real KPI.
- **failureMode:** The match is wrong or thin ("you both lived in Yorkshire") and the pairing feels forced and patronising. Or the shared place carries a bad memory (a bombed street, a workhouse) for one of them.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** Two Chairs, One Photo

## 3. Pass the Photo

- **oneLine:** You look at your picture, then the tablet says "your turn" with a big arrow and you hand it to the person next to you.
- **inputNeeded:** Nothing beyond each participant's existing photo set; coordinator picks who is in the circle at the start.
- **interaction:** Physically hand the tablet sideways. No screen input at all except an optional tap to advance.
- **whyItMightWork:** Procedural memory for passing things round a table (cards, plates, collection plates) is intact long after episodic memory fails. The handover creates an unforced turn structure without anyone having to police it, freeing the coordinator to sit back.
- **failureMode:** A resident won't let go of the tablet, or drops it. Someone gets skipped and reads it as exclusion. The "your turn" prompt feels like being put on the spot in front of a group.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** Two Chairs, One Photo

## 4. Lounge Wall (Ambient Slideshow)

- **oneLine:** Photos from residents' lives drift slowly across the big screen in the lounge all afternoon, and you can look up whenever you feel like it.
- **inputNeeded:** Per-photo family consent flag for "may be shown in communal areas"; ideally 20+ photos across the home to avoid repetition.
- **interaction:** No input at all. Ever. Optionally a staff member taps to hold a picture that has caught someone's eye.
- **whyItMightWork:** Zero-demand exposure — no task, no failure possible. Repeated ambient exposure functions as priming, so the same image in a later one-to-one session is recognised faster and more confidently (errorless learning by pre-familiarisation). Seeds spontaneous unstructured conversation, which is where coordinators get their best moments.
- **failureMode:** A resident sees a photo of a deceased spouse without warning and in public. Or sees another family's photos and becomes confused about whether that is their own family. Or the wall becomes wallpaper nobody looks at.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** novel

## 5. My Turn to Show You

- **oneLine:** You are the one holding the tablet, showing your own photos to a visitor or another resident, and the tablet quietly gives them things to ask you about.
- **inputNeeded:** 8–15 photos with one line of family-written context each ("Dad's fishing boat, Whitby, about 1962") — the context is shown to the *listener*, not the patient.
- **interaction:** Tap to move to the next photo. Speak aloud as much or as little as you like.
- **whyItMightWork:** Inverts the expertise gradient — the resident is the authority in the room and the visitor is the ignorant one. Cue cards go to the person with intact memory, so the resident is never quizzed. Emotional salience plus social reward; dignity is structurally built in rather than tacked on.
- **failureMode:** The visitor takes over, reads the cue aloud as a question, and it becomes a test after all. Or the resident cannot produce the story and feels the failure more sharply because they were cast as the expert.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** novel

## 6. The Grandchild's Errand

- **oneLine:** A child or grandchild visiting sits with you and the tablet gives *them* three small jobs — find out who is in this photo, get Grandma to hum this tune, take a picture of you both.
- **inputNeeded:** Family flags 3–5 photos as "unidentified" or "we'd love the story"; a guest-mode entry the visitor can open without a login.
- **interaction:** Talk. The grandchild does all the tapping and recording.
- **whyItMightWork:** Gives the visitor a script, which is the single biggest barrier to good visits — relatives run out of things to say in eight minutes and leave early. Converts the visit into content capture. Autobiographical retrieval is warmest when prompted by a loved young face rather than a screen.
- **failureMode:** The child treats it as a quiz and gets visibly disappointed when Grandma doesn't know. The resident senses homework being done on them. Recording feels like surveillance.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** My Turn to Show You

## 7. The Voice Letterbox

- **oneLine:** A little envelope appears at the start of your session; you tap it and hear your daughter's voice saying hello, and if you want you can just talk back and she'll hear it tonight.
- **inputNeeded:** A 10–30 second voice clip from a named family member, ideally refreshed weekly; family app with a one-tap record-and-send.
- **interaction:** Tap one large envelope. Speak aloud — no record button, no stop button; the app listens for the pause.
- **whyItMightWork:** Voice carries emotional salience and social presence far better than text or even photos, and familiar voices are recognised long after faces. Removes the "how do I work a phone" barrier to family contact entirely. The reply loop makes the resident a sender, not only a recipient.
- **failureMode:** No letter arrives today and the empty letterbox reads as abandonment — worse than never having had one. Hearing a voice without the person present causes searching behaviour ("where is she? is she here?"). Recorded replies that are rambling or distressed are seen by family and cause guilt.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** novel

## 8. Hearts From Home

- **oneLine:** While you are looking at a photo, small hearts and a name drift up the corner of the screen because your son, wherever he is, is watching along.
- **inputNeeded:** Nothing new — family app shows a mirror of the current session and a single "send love" button.
- **interaction:** No input. Purely received.
- **whyItMightWork:** Social presence without conversational demand — the resident is accompanied, not asked anything. Distant relatives desperately want a low-effort way to participate; giving them one increases the volume of content they supply. Positive affect during a session improves engagement in the next.
- **failureMode:** Nobody sends anything and the absence is legible. The resident tries to talk to the hearts and gets no answer. Ambient movement on screen is visually confusing for people with attention or visual-processing difficulty.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** The Voice Letterbox

## 9. Recognised Today

- **oneLine:** (Patient-facing half) At the end of a session you are told, warmly, that your granddaughter has been told you looked at her wedding photo — and next time there's a thank-you from her waiting.
- **inputNeeded:** Nothing new — derived; family opts in to receiving a daily digest.
- **interaction:** Just watch, then optionally tap once to "send it".
- **whyItMightWork:** Closes the loop that keeps families supplying content: the reason relatives stop uploading is that nothing comes back. Framed to the patient as *giving news to family*, not as a report card on their performance. Anticipated reciprocity is a strong motivator even when the specific exchange isn't remembered.
- **failureMode:** Catastrophic if it ever reads as a score ("you got 4 of 7 right, we told your family"). Family receive a bad day's digest and grieve. Patient asks the staff to send it and it has already gone, causing repetition distress.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** novel

## 10. Life Bingo

- **oneLine:** The coordinator reads out "who worked on the railways?" to the whole lounge, and if that's you, you put your hand up and everyone claps.
- **inputNeeded:** A short structured life-facts sheet per resident (jobs, hometowns, number of children, instruments played, countries visited) — 10 checkbox-style facts, no photos needed.
- **interaction:** Raise a hand or speak. Staff taps the answer on the big screen; residents touch nothing.
- **whyItMightWork:** Semantic and self-referential facts survive far longer than episodic detail, so hit rates are high — deliberate errorless design. Group applause is a strong, immediate social reward. One staff member runs twenty residents at once, which is the actual staffing reality.
- **failureMode:** A resident whose life-sheet is thin (no family, no notable job) is never called and sits through their own invisibility. A fact is wrong or out of date (a spouse who has died, a job they were ashamed of) and is announced publicly.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** novel

## 11. The Song Everyone Knows

- **oneLine:** A song from when you were twenty starts playing in the lounge, the words come up huge on the screen, and whoever wants to sings.
- **inputNeeded:** Year of birth per resident (that's genuinely enough); optionally 3–5 family-named songs per person, plus a licensed period music library.
- **interaction:** Sing, hum, tap a foot, or nothing at all. No screen input.
- **whyItMightWork:** Music from ages 15–25 (the reminiscence bump) is stored with unusual robustness and is often intact when speech is not. Singing is procedural, not recall — people who cannot say their own name can complete a lyric. Group singing produces measurable mood and arousal changes across a room.
- **failureMode:** A song is strongly tied to a bereavement and the resident weeps in public with no way to opt out gracefully. Volume distresses residents with hearing aids. Non-singers feel conspicuous.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** novel

## 12. Can You Help Me With This One?

- **oneLine:** Someone else's old photo comes up and you are asked, genuinely, whether you happen to know what kind of car that is or where that seaside might be.
- **inputNeeded:** Photos any family has flagged as "we don't know where/when this is"; consent to show outside the owning family.
- **interaction:** Speak an answer aloud, or tap one of two large options. Staff or the app captures it.
- **whyItMightWork:** Reframes the entire interaction from being helped to helping. Targets semantic memory (car marques, ship types, cinema fronts, regimental badges) which is often robust and is exactly the knowledge a younger person lacks. Contribution to another person's life story is a powerful, non-infantilising motivator.
- **failureMode:** The resident realises their answer is not really needed and feels humoured. They give a confidently wrong answer that gets attached to another family's photo. Being asked something unanswerable in a "helper" frame is a harder fall than failing a game.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** My Turn to Show You

## 13. The Welcome Book

- **oneLine:** When you're new, the lounge screen shows a few of your photos and one thing you're proud of, and people come over and talk to you about them.
- **inputNeeded:** On admission, family supplies 5 photos, a job/hometown line, and one "thing to ask them about" — a single onboarding form.
- **interaction:** None by the patient. Others approach and talk.
- **whyItMightWork:** Directly attacks the worst window in care-home life — the first fortnight, when relocation stress and unfamiliarity spike confusion and withdrawal. Gives staff and peers a conversational handle instantly. Identity is asserted publicly before the institution overwrites it.
- **failureMode:** Being displayed without understanding why; feels like a specimen board. A resident who is ashamed of the move has their past broadcast. Other residents' misremembering ("I knew your husband") causes confabulated confusion.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** Lounge Wall

## 14. Two Hands, One Pair

- **oneLine:** You hold one half of a matching pair and the person opposite holds the other, and together you decide whether the two pictures are the same person.
- **inputNeeded:** 3+ photos of the same person across decades, per resident; or same-place-different-era pairs.
- **interaction:** Two tablets, or one screen split down the middle. Each person taps one large "yes, same" / "no, different" — the answer only resolves when both have tapped.
- **whyItMightWork:** Recognition (not recall) plus enforced collaboration: neither can complete it alone, so neither is individually wrong. Cross-decade face matching exercises perceptual identity linking, which is what fails when a spouse becomes unrecognisable. Joint attention is protective against withdrawal.
- **failureMode:** They disagree and one is overruled — public contradiction is exactly the humiliation to avoid. One partner is non-verbal or asleep and the other is stranded waiting. Requires two people simultaneously available, which care homes rarely have.
- **difficultyToBuild:** high
- **variantOf:** Two Chairs, One Photo

## 15. The Empty Chair (Scaffolded Video Call)

- **oneLine:** Your son appears on the big screen and talks you through the photos he took at the beach, and you just sit and watch him.
- **inputNeeded:** A scheduled slot; the family member's own photo set. The app feeds prompt cards to the *relative's* screen ("show her the dog first — she reacted well last week").
- **interaction:** Watch and speak. Staff starts the call; the resident touches nothing.
- **whyItMightWork:** Video calls with dementia usually fail because the relative doesn't know what to say and asks memory-testing questions ("do you remember me?"). Coaching the relative in real time converts the call into shared looking, which is sustainable for 20 minutes rather than 3. Face-plus-voice maximises recognition.
- **failureMode:** The resident doesn't grasp that the screen is a live person, tries to touch them, and becomes agitated. Freezing or lag reads as the person being upset or leaving. The call ends and the loss is felt fresh each time.
- **difficultyToBuild:** high
- **variantOf:** The Voice Letterbox

## 16. Postbag Between Homes

- **oneLine:** A man in a care home in Glasgow has sent a photo of the shipyard he worked in, and it's on your screen this morning because you worked in one too.
- **inputNeeded:** Nothing new from either family — matched on existing place/occupation/era tags across homes. Requires inter-home consent framework.
- **interaction:** Watch; optionally speak a one-line reply that gets sent onward.
- **whyItMightWork:** Peer-to-peer reminiscence at scale, sidestepping the small-N problem of a single home where nobody shares your background. Being written *to* by a stranger who shares your world confers status and novelty without demanding recall. Correspondence is a familiar lifelong social form for this cohort.
- **failureMode:** Confusion about who this stranger is and whether they are expected to know them. A correspondent dies and the exchange stops with no explicable reason. Consent and safeguarding across institutions is genuinely hairy.
- **difficultyToBuild:** high
- **variantOf:** Same Town, Same Time

## 17. The Legacy Recording

- **oneLine:** You are asked to tell a story to your great-granddaughter, who is three, so she'll have it when she's older — and the tablet just listens.
- **inputNeeded:** A named recipient (photo + first name) and 5–10 prompt topics chosen by family ("tell her about the day you met Grandad").
- **interaction:** Speak aloud to a photo of the recipient on screen. No buttons; staff or family starts it.
- **whyItMightWork:** Generativity — the drive to leave something behind is intact and strong in late life, and it reframes the activity as legacy rather than therapy. There is no correct answer, so failure is impossible by construction. Speaking to a specific loved face produces far richer narrative than speaking to a prompt.
- **failureMode:** The resident cannot produce a story and experiences it as failing a descendant, which is heavier than failing a game. Distressing material surfaces with no one present to hold it. Family later hear a recording that shows decline and find it painful.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** My Turn to Show You

## 18. The Handover Card

- **oneLine:** (Staff-facing; the patient experiences only its effect) A carer you've never met walks in and already knows to ask you about the greenhouse.
- **inputNeeded:** Nothing new — generated from what landed well in recent sessions, plus a one-tap staff note.
- **interaction:** None by the patient. Staff tap once on a lounge tablet or read a printed card at shift start.
- **whyItMightWork:** The activities coordinator's real constraint is time and turnover; a 15-second read that makes the next interaction land is the highest-leverage thing in the building. For the resident it produces continuity of being known — the single strongest protective factor against institutional depersonalisation.
- **failureMode:** Reduces a person to three bullet points that every carer then repeats, so the resident is asked about the greenhouse nine times a day. Stale or wrong notes ("ask about her husband" — he died in March). Feels like a chart, not a person.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** Recognised Today

## 19. Night Chair

- **oneLine:** It's the small hours, you're awake and unsettled, and a night carer sets a quiet screen beside you where your wife's voice reads out something ordinary and familiar.
- **inputNeeded:** A 3–5 minute calm audio recording by a familiar voice (reading, humming, describing a routine day) plus 10–20 low-arousal photos — explicitly no faces of the deceased unless family confirms.
- **interaction:** None. Watch and listen. Staff starts it and walks away.
- **whyItMightWork:** Sundowning and nocturnal agitation are the highest-burden hours for night staff and the most distressing for residents. Familiar-voice audio is a validated soothing input; zero cognitive demand means no possibility of failure at the worst-functioning time of day. Gives one night carer a way to settle a resident without medication.
- **failureMode:** The voice makes them search the room for a person who isn't there, escalating rather than settling. Screen light disrupts sleep. Used as a substitute for a human being sitting with them.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** Lounge Wall

## 20. Table of Objects

- **oneLine:** Real things are laid on the table — a coin, a hairbrush, a tobacco tin — and when you pick one up the screen beside you shows the photo from your own life that matches it.
- **inputNeeded:** Family-tagged photos against a fixed vocabulary of ~30 physical props the home keeps in a box; plus the physical props themselves.
- **interaction:** Pick up a real object with your hands. Nothing is tapped.
- **whyItMightWork:** Tactile and olfactory cues bypass the screen entirely and reach memory routes that images don't. Handling objects is procedural and dignified; it looks like life, not like therapy. In a group, the object circulates and each person's screen shows *their own* matching memory — parallel personalisation from one shared prop.
- **failureMode:** An object triggers something dark (a hospital item, a wedding ring) with no warning. Object goes in a mouth or a pocket. The technology tie-in (NFC tags in props) fails and a carer is left debugging in front of residents.
- **difficultyToBuild:** high
- **variantOf:** novel

## Cross-cutting notes for the culling board

- **Never a score, never in public.** Anything numeric leaking into a lounge context is a dignity failure regardless of clinical value. Researcher-facing data must be invisible at the table.
- **Bereavement flags are load-bearing.** Almost every failure mode above is a variant of "an unflagged dead person appeared". A per-person, per-photo sensitivity flag maintained by family is a prerequisite for at least twelve of these.
- **Staff time is the binding constraint, not tablet count.** Mechanics that need one staff action to serve many residents (Life Bingo, Song Everyone Knows, Lounge Wall, Handover Card) will survive contact with a real rota. Mechanics needing two simultaneously available, matched residents (Two Hands One Pair) probably won't, even if they're clinically the most interesting.
- **The family-facing loop is the content engine.** Recognised Today and Hearts From Home aren't patient mechanics so much as the supply chain for every other mechanic on this list.
