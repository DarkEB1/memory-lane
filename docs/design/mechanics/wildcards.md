# Wildcards

**Lens:** the odd board. Everything the other seven produced is a screen doing a screen-shaped thing. This board borrows its forms from places that are not software and were never meant to be: repertory theatre, a stately-home audio handset, the Shipping Forecast, a bus bell, a market barrow, a panto matinee, a fishing float, a switchboard, a lost property counter, a bulb on a windowsill, a parcel that needs signing for, a village fête. The wager is that a dementia patient does not need to learn any of these — they already own the script. An 85-year-old has signed for a parcel ten thousand times, rung a bus bell, shouted "he's behind you", licked a pencil before writing on a betting slip. Those scripts are procedural, over-learned, and socially scaffolded: they tell you what to do with your body without asking you to remember anything. Put the family's own photographs and voices *inside* one of those scripts and the memory work happens sideways, while the patient is busy doing something they know how to do.

Two rules held across the board. First, **the patient must be the one with the standing** — the host, the expert, the customer, the operator, the person the stranger is ringing for help. Never the examinee. Second, **the frame absorbs the failure**: if you cannot remember, the bus goes past the stop anyway, the stall-holder wraps it anyway, the scene partner says the line for you the way an old pro covers for someone drying on stage. Nothing here can end on a failure because in none of these forms does failure exist as a category.

### 1. The Serial

**One line:** At the same time every day a four-minute radio drama plays the next episode of a story about the patient's own life, opening with "previously, on Ashfield Road…" and stopping dead on a cliffhanger.

- **Input needed:** One life period broken by the family into 15-30 episode beats (a courtship, the war years, the move to the new house, the first shop) — each beat one or two sentences of what happened, plus any photos and a name list. A relative optionally records the narrator; otherwise TTS with a period announcer voice. Family adds beats a few at a time, not all at once.
- **Interaction:** Just listen and watch. One large button meaning "again from the start of today's".
- **Why it might work:** The "previously on" recap is spaced retrieval disguised as a broadcasting convention — it re-presents yesterday's material at 24-hour intervals with zero retrieval demand and zero possibility of error (errorless re-exposure). Serial form manufactures anticipation, which is a motivational structure that does not require episodic memory to function: you do not need to recall the cliffhanger to feel the pull of tomorrow's episode when it arrives. Autobiographical content is delivered in third person, which lets a patient who no longer recognises the events as their own still enjoy them as a story.
- **Failure mode:** Hearing your own life narrated by a stranger's voice in the third person can feel like being written about after death, or like being told about someone else's life while sensing it should be yours and it isn't. A cliffhanger on a genuinely painful beat (an illness, a death, a wartime loss) left hanging for 24 hours is cruel. Distress is likely if the drama reaches a bereavement the patient does not currently hold.
- **Difficulty:** high
- **Variant of:** novel

### 2. Cue Script

**One line:** The patient is handed a two-hander scene in which only their own lines are printed — large, in a proper actor's part — and their daughter's recorded voice plays the other role, feeding them the cue and waiting for them to come in.

- **Input needed:** 3-10 short scenes (6-12 lines each) written from real remembered exchanges — the thing the patient always said, family arguments about the dog, the way they answered the phone at the shop. A family member records the *other* character's lines with natural pauses. The patient's lines are typed only.
- **Interaction:** Read aloud from large type when the cue lands. No buttons. If they say nothing, the scene partner covers ("…well, you always said it was the gate") and moves on.
- **Why it might work:** Reading aloud is preserved far longer than recall — this is oral reading with a socially compelling reason to do it, so it drills fluent speech production without ever asking for retrieval. The cue-script form (a real repertory device: actors were given only their own part and the three words before it) means the patient never sees the other person's line, so it does not read as a script to be studied but as a conversation to be had. Prosody and rhythm carry a lot of autobiographical charge; hearing your own catchphrase in your own mouth is identity-affirming rather than test-like.
- **Failure mode:** If the patient cannot read fluently any more it collapses immediately and visibly. Reading your own remembered words with no memory of ever saying them is a specific and horrible flavour of estrangement. Also: family will write scenes that flatter themselves.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** novel

### 3. The Audio Guide

**One line:** The patient holds a chunky numbered handset, like the ones at stately homes, and their own front room — the sideboard, the clock, the picture over the fire — is the exhibition; press 14 and a curator's voice tells them about the object at number 14.

- **Input needed:** 8-20 photographs of objects and corners of the patient's *actual home* (past or present), each with a printed number, plus a 20-45 second commentary per object recorded by a relative in the flat, factual, faintly reverent register of a museum guide ("The clock on the mantelpiece was a retirement present from the depot in 1979…").
- **Interaction:** Press large numbered buttons on screen, or on a real numbered keypad accessory. Wrong numbers are not wrong — they just play a different exhibit.
- **Why it might work:** Museum-guide framing inverts the status relationship: the exhibition is *about them*, treats their objects as historically significant, and speaks about their life in the register reserved for things that matter. Number-entry is an over-learned motor script with no memory load and no possibility of error, and it gives agency and pacing to the patient rather than to the app. Object-cued autobiographical retrieval is strong and durable, and objects (unlike faces) rarely carry the shame of not being recognised.
- **Failure mode:** Museums are for the dead and the finished. If the framing lands as "your life is now an exhibit", it can read as elegy. Patients may also try to walk to the object and not find it (particularly in a care home where the objects are in a house they left).
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** novel

### 4. Signed For

**One line:** A parcel arrives on the screen with the patient's name and old address on it, and they sign for it with a finger, and then it opens and there is something from their life inside.

- **Input needed:** Nothing new — draws from existing photos, voice clips, songs. Enriched by the patient's full name as it was written on post, and one or two former addresses, and ideally a relative's note of what a real delivery to that house looked like.
- **Interaction:** Drag a finger across a signature strip. Any mark counts as a signature — a scrawl, a dot, a tremor line, nothing at all after ten seconds.
- **Why it might work:** Signing your name is one of the most over-learned graphomotor programmes a person owns; it survives deep into the disease and is produced without deliberation. It is also an act of legal personhood — you sign because you are the person entitled to receive this. Seeing your own name and your own old address in an envelope window is a powerful, non-interrogative autobiographical cue. And the delivery frame gives a completely natural reason for a surprise to arrive daily without anyone asking a question.
- **Failure mode:** Signing is bound up with capacity, and a patient who has recently been through power-of-attorney conversations may be frightened of putting their name to anything. A signature that comes out as an unrecognisable scribble is a private, visible confrontation with decline. Some will worry about what they have agreed to.
- **Difficulty:** low
- **Variant of:** novel

### 5. The Bell Cord

**One line:** The patient sits upstairs on a bus that drives, slowly and in real time, along a route they rode for twenty years, and when they see their stop coming they reach up and ring the bell, and the bus pulls in.

- **Input needed:** One route the patient travelled habitually before roughly age 60 — start, end, and the stop they used — supplied as place names by the family. Imagery derived from street-level map services and archive photography; family may add 3-5 photos of buildings along the way.
- **Interaction:** Pull a cord, or press one enormous bell strip that runs the whole width of the screen above head height. Ringing at any point is fine — the bus stops, and if it wasn't their stop it simply drives on afterwards.
- **Why it might work:** Route knowledge and stop-recognition are spatial, procedural and rehearsed thousands of times — a very different and better-preserved system than naming. The bell is an act of intent with a physical consequence and an unmistakable sound, which gives an immediate, legible sense of agency to someone who may have very little. Motion through a familiar streetscape produces spontaneous autobiographical retrieval without any question being asked ("that's where the baths were").
- **Failure mode:** Ringing and finding it isn't your stop is a recognisable public humiliation for anyone who has ever done it for real. Buses are also where getting lost happens — the scenario can trigger travel anxiety, or a belief that they must now get off and get home. Motion on screen can nauseate.
- **Difficulty:** high
- **Variant of:** a distant cousin of Walk Down Your Street, but recognition-and-bell rather than recall-and-advance

### 6. The Market Stall

**One line:** A barrow-boy in a market shouts his wares — and the wares are the things from the patient's own kitchen and the patient's own life — and when they point at one he wraps it in newspaper, tells them what it's worth in old money, and hands it over.

- **Input needed:** 6-15 photographs of objects the patient owned or handled (the Toby jug, the Pyrex, the bike, the good scissors), plus one line of family context each. The patter is generated; a family member can record the stall-holder if they have the nerve.
- **Interaction:** Point at one of two or three large objects on the barrow. That's it — no money, no counting, no basket.
- **Why it might work:** Market patter is rhythmic, loud, funny and expects nothing back — a form that carries attention without demanding it. The patient's role is *customer*, which is a position of choice and status, and choosing is a preserved capacity long after naming is not. Objects are recognised readily and carry dense autobiographical associations; the wrapping-and-handing-over gives a small completion ritual per item so every single round ends in acquisition rather than assessment.
- **Failure mode:** Some patients will try to pay and become distressed about money they cannot find, or believe they have spent money they don't have — financial anxiety is extremely common. Fast loud patter can overwhelm someone with hearing loss or agitation, and the joviality can feel like being talked at.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** novel (adjacent to What's It Worth? but the patient buys rather than appraises)

### 7. The Float

**One line:** The patient sits and watches a fishing float on still water for as long as they like, and every so often it dips — and when they strike, they land a photograph, a voice, a song from their life, and then the water goes still again.

- **Input needed:** Nothing — assembled from whatever exists. Enriched by a location the patient fished, walked or sat beside, and by any ambient recording of real water.
- **Interaction:** One tap anywhere when the float dips. Tapping when nothing is happening is fine (you thought you saw something — everyone does). Not tapping at all is fine; the fish comes off, another comes along.
- **Why it might work:** This is the only mechanic on the board built around *waiting*, which is a preserved and even pleasurable capacity, and it converts sustained low-arousal attention into a legitimate activity rather than an empty one. The variable-interval reward schedule is the most attention-sustaining schedule known and requires no memory whatsoever. Each catch is a memory cue delivered at a moment of alertness and mild excitement — arousal at encoding, which helps. And missing a bite is universally, unremarkably normal.
- **Failure mode:** The metaphor is dead in the water for anyone who never fished, and reads as pointless or babyish. Waiting can turn into anxiety or a sense of being left with nothing. Someone with a slowed reaction time may miss every bite and, despite the framing, conclude they are too slow now.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** novel

### 8. The Bulb on the Windowsill

**One line:** A relative plants a real bulb in a real pot on the patient's windowsill, and every day the tablet shows the patient one photograph of the person that bulb belongs to — and when it flowers, weeks later, that person comes.

- **Input needed:** A real bulb, pot and windowsill (posted as a kit, or planted by a visitor), plus 20-40 photographs and voice clips of ONE nominated person, and a genuine committed visit date roughly aligned to flowering.
- **Interaction:** No screen input at all. Optionally water the real plant with a real jug.
- **Why it might work:** The plant is an external, physical, non-digital memory prosthesis for a future intention — prospective memory is catastrophically impaired, but a visibly growing object in the room does the remembering on the patient's behalf and can be re-read at a glance, endlessly, without asking anyone. Tending it is a lifelong procedural routine with intrinsic worth. And it concentrates all the app's exposure on one person during the growth period, which is the right shape for making a face survive: massed, sustained, single-target exposure with a real reunion as the payoff.
- **Failure mode:** The visit does not happen, or the bulb dies, or somebody moves the pot. Either failure is concrete and heartbreaking and is sitting in the room. In a care home the pot will be moved. There is also a bleak reading available — the plant flowering as a countdown.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** a physical, long-horizon cousin of The Kept Promise

### 9. The Shipping Forecast

**One line:** At the same hour every evening a calm, formal, unhurried bulletin is read out in the cadence of the Shipping Forecast — but the areas are the places of the patient's life and the report is who came today, who comes tomorrow, and what the weather did — and then it ends and the room is quiet.

- **Input needed:** The patient's place names (3-6: birthplace, the street, the works, the seaside they went to), the visit rota, and a locale for real weather. Optionally a relative records the bulletin format once and the app splices names into it; otherwise a deliberately flat, formal TTS voice.
- **Interaction:** No input at all, ever. It reads, it ends.
- **Why it might work:** The Shipping Forecast is the purest ritual object British radio ever made: fixed time, fixed order, fixed cadence, meaningless to most listeners and profoundly settling to all of them. Predictable prosodic structure is itself a memory scaffold — the *shape* is learned even when the content is not, so the patient knows where they are in the day from the sound alone. It delivers real orientation information (day, weather, who is coming) inside a form that carries no implication that anyone has forgotten anything.
- **Failure mode:** Utterly opaque to anyone outside that cultural reference — and even inside it, the association is with the last thing you hear before you're alone in the dark. Delivering "nobody is coming tomorrow" in an unemotional liturgical voice is bleak. Sundowning agitation is worst at exactly this hour.
- **Difficulty:** low
- **Variant of:** novel

### 10. He's Behind You

**One line:** A pantomime plays out on the lounge screen with the patient's own family recorded as the cast, and at the moment everyone knows is coming, the whole room shouts the line — and so does the patient, because the line is not a memory, it's a reflex.

- **Input needed:** A short scripted panto scene (supplied by the app) with 4-8 slots for family-recorded lines and family photographs cut in as the characters; ideally 2-4 relatives recording 15-30 seconds each, hammily. Works in a care home lounge with several residents.
- **Interaction:** Shout at the screen. Nothing is detected, nothing is scored, and the show carries on either way.
- **Why it might work:** Panto call-and-response is culturally installed at about age five and never leaves; it is a group vocal reflex, not a recall act, and it is one of the very few situations in which an adult may safely shout in a room full of people. The social contagion of a whole lounge shouting pulls in people who initiate nothing on their own. Seeing your own son in a wig being booed is high-emotional-salience, funny, and reverses the pity dynamic entirely.
- **Failure mode:** A resident who does not join in is now visibly the one not joining in, in a room. Loud, chaotic and boo-ing content can frighten or agitate. And it depends entirely on whether the family will make fools of themselves on camera — many won't, and a half-hearted panto is a sad thing.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** novel

### 11. The Sunday Table

**One line:** An empty laid table appears on the screen, seen from the head of it, and the patient is asked — as the host, because it is their table — to put everybody where they sit.

- **Input needed:** 5-10 face photographs of the people who genuinely sat around that table, plus a family note of the actual habitual seating if anyone remembers it (used only to make the app agree, never to correct).
- **Interaction:** Drag a face to a chair, with very generous snapping. Any face goes in any chair and stays there. When the table is full, the meal arrives and everybody says hello in their own voice.
- **Why it might work:** Habitual seating is spatial, over-learned and socially encoded — a different retrieval route than naming, and one that often survives when names are gone. More important is the role reversal: hosting is an act of authority and hospitality, and the patient is being asked to *arrange* their family, not identify them. There is no correct arrangement the app will assert, so every table is right, and the reward is the whole family greeting them by voice at once.
- **Failure mode:** Empty chairs, and who is missing from the photographs, and the person who used to sit at the other end. Laying a table for people who are dead is a real and foreseeable collision. Drag-and-drop is the single hardest gesture for tremor and arthritis even with snapping.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** novel

### 12. The Lost Property Office

**One line:** A clerk at a counter brings out things that have been handed in — a photograph, a hat, a watch, a song playing faintly from a box — and asks the patient, respectfully, whether any of them are theirs to claim.

- **Input needed:** Any existing photos and objects, plus a handful of era-matched decoys that are plausibly of the period but not from this life. Family flags which items are definitely theirs; the app never contradicts a claim either way.
- **Interaction:** Tap "that's mine" or "not mine" — two large cards. Whatever they say, the clerk writes it in the ledger, wraps it, and hands it over if claimed.
- **Why it might work:** "Is this yours?" is a fundamentally different question from "what is this?" — it asks for a feeling of familiarity rather than a name or an episode, and familiarity-based recognition is preserved much longer than recollection. Claiming is an assertion of ownership and identity, and the lost-property frame supplies a socially normal reason not to be sure. The clerk's deference does a lot of quiet work on dignity.
- **Failure mode:** The whole conceit is that things have been lost, which is close to the bone. A patient may disclaim something that is genuinely, importantly theirs and a family member watching will find that unbearable. Others may claim everything and then be distressed about where their possessions have gone.
- **Difficulty:** low
- **Variant of:** novel

### 13. The Switchboard

**One line:** A little telephone exchange lights up in front of the patient, a voice in the earpiece says "put me through to our Kathleen, would you", and the patient pushes the plug into the socket with Kathleen's face on it and the two of them start talking.

- **Input needed:** 4-8 people, each with a face photo and a 10-20 second clip of them talking to *someone else* (not to the patient), plus one recorded "caller" line per connection asking to be put through by name and relationship.
- **Interaction:** Drag a fat plug into one of four or five large sockets, or just tap a socket. Any socket connects someone to someone; nobody is ever refused.
- **Why it might work:** The task is name-to-face matching, but the patient is the operator rather than the subject — connecting other people, doing a job, providing a service. Occupational role-play recruits procedural and identity-level memory rather than episodic. Hearing two relatives talk to each other while the patient listens in is social presence without any conversational demand at all, which is precisely what a patient with word-finding difficulty needs and almost never gets.
- **Failure mode:** Plugs and sockets require aim, which tremor punishes. Overhearing family talk to each other can read as being talked about, or excluded. Anyone who actually worked an exchange may find a bad simulation insulting; anyone who didn't may not understand the object at all.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** novel

### 14. Guess the Weight of the Cake

**One line:** A cake sits under a glass dome at the village fête and the patient writes a guess at its weight on a slip — and so do their son, their granddaughter and the man from number nine, and nobody knows the answer until Sunday.

- **Input needed:** A weekly unknowable question set by the family that no living person can currently answer or verify quickly ("how heavy is this cake", "how many jars in the picture", "what year was this photo taken"), with an accompanying photo. Family members must actually submit their own guesses through the family app.
- **Interaction:** Slide a big chunky pointer along a dial, or tap one of three number cards. Then wait.
- **Why it might work:** It is structurally impossible to be wrong in a way that reveals anything about you, because nobody knows the answer and everybody else has guessed too — which makes it the only genuinely non-diagnostic guessing game available. Delayed reveal creates anticipation across days without any prospective-memory requirement (the app brings the reveal to them). Being listed on a slate alongside the family is horizontal participation, not care.
- **Failure mode:** The waiting means nothing happens now, which for a patient with no sense of elapsed time is just an absence. If family members don't submit guesses the slate is empty and the patient is alone at the fête. Numbers and weights may be meaningless to someone with impaired numeracy.
- **Difficulty:** low
- **Variant of:** novel

### 15. The Wrong Number

**One line:** The telephone rings and it's a stranger who has dialled wrong — a young woman lost trying to find the market in the town where the patient grew up — and she asks them, because they'd know, how you get there from the station.

- **Input needed:** The patient's home town or working locality and 3-5 places within it they knew well (family supplies place names only), plus a bank of recorded "caller" scripts the app fits the place names into. No correctness data — the caller thanks them for whatever they say.
- **Interaction:** Lift a handset or tap a ringing phone, then speak aloud. Saying nothing is met with the caller apologising for troubling them and ringing off warmly.
- **Why it might work:** The patient is the one being *asked for help*, by someone with less knowledge than them, about the domain in which their knowledge is strongest and most durable (remote spatial and semantic memory of a childhood locality). Helping a stranger is a social script that runs automatically and requires no memory of the app, the day, or the previous call. Word-finding is far easier in service of a purpose than under examination. And a stranger has no expectations to disappoint.
- **Failure mode:** It is a small, kind lie, and a patient who works out that nobody actually rang may feel gulled. Others may believe it entirely and become anxious about the lost young woman, or try to go and help. Cold-call framing frightens some older people badly.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** a role-inverted variant of Who's on the Line? / The Ringing Telephone

### 16. In Her Own Hand

**One line:** The patient's own recipe card is on the screen in their own handwriting, and their daughter's voice reads it out one line at a time while the patient actually stirs the actual bowl in front of them.

- **Input needed:** Photographs or scans of 3-10 pages of the patient's *own handwriting* — a recipe card, a shopping list, a diary page, the back of a photograph — plus a family member reading each one aloud, and (for the cooking variant) a real bowl, spoon and ingredients set up by a carer or visitor.
- **Interaction:** No screen input while cooking — the app advances one step at a time on a long timer, or on the sound of a spoon. One enormous "say that again" button.
- **Why it might work:** A person's own handwriting is a perceptual object with an identity charge nothing else has, and it is *recognised* rather than recalled. Pairing it with the motor sequence it describes recruits procedural memory (mixing, kneading, folding) which is largely spared, while the read-aloud line-by-line structure is errorless: the answer is always given before it is needed, so there is nothing to fail. Producing something real and edible at the end is an outcome the patient can see, hold, and give to somebody else.
- **Failure mode:** Requires a person physically present, which is the scarcest resource in the entire product. Kitchen safety is a genuine risk and the app can only pretend it isn't. And seeing your own confident handwriting from 1971 while your hands now shake is a direct, unmediated comparison with who you were.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** a physical-media cousin of Tea and Toast and Hands Remember

## Notes for the culling board

The three most likely to survive contact with reality: **Signed For** (very low build cost, brilliant procedural hook, delivers any content), **The Lost Property Office** (recognition-without-naming is the single most valuable question shape in this whole pool), and **The Shipping Forecast** (a fixed daily ritual that carries real orientation data and costs almost nothing to build).

The three most likely to be culled and worth culling last: **The Serial** (highest authoring burden of anything on any board, and the bereavement risk is real, but it is also the only mechanic here that could make someone *want tomorrow to come*), **The Bulb on the Windowsill** (depends on a promise being kept, which is exactly the thing families cannot guarantee), and **The Bell Cord** (expensive, motion-sickness-prone, and dependent on street imagery that may simply not exist for a 1940s childhood).

The one I would fight for hardest is **Cue Script** — it is the only mechanic in this entire pool that gets the patient speaking fluently at length without asking them to remember a single thing, and dementia takes speech before it takes personality.
