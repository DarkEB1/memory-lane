# Mechanics — The Game Designer's Lens

**Brief:** Build life-content memory exercises that feel like the games this generation grew up with — card tables, dominoes, bingo halls, the wireless, church fetes, Sunday jigsaws, quiz shows, sing-alongs at the piano, guessing games in the snug. Not like a computer test.

**Design principles applied throughout:**

- **Luck protects dignity.** If a die, a shuffle or a tombola drum decides the outcome, a bad round is bad luck — not evidence of decline. Wherever a mechanic could feel like a test, a chance element is layered on top so the patient always has an honourable explanation for any outcome.
- **Anticipation is the pleasure, not the answer.** The moment before the card turns is worth more than the card. Pacing and reveal do most of the emotional work.
- **The hand should enjoy itself.** Weight, snap, slide, thunk. Procedural and motor pleasure survives when episodic memory does not.
- **No clocks, no scores against a norm, no red.** Ever. A timer is a cruelty engine.
- **Every round ends well.** The mechanic must be structurally incapable of ending on a failure.

Two mechanics below are **purely delightful with no testing component whatsoever** (The Wireless, The Slide Carousel), plus three more that test nothing but are shaped like play (Shove-Ha'penny Memory Lane, The Window Seat, Four-Piece Jigsaw).

## 1. Family Snap

**One line:** Cards flip over one at a time in the middle of the table and when two faces in a row are the same person, the patient slaps the big brass bell and everyone's photo cheers.

**Input needed:** 8+ photos of family members, at least 2 distinct photos per person, 4+ people. Optionally a 2-second cheer/laugh clip per person.

**Interaction:** One enormous physical-feeling bell-push button, roughly the size of a saucer, in the centre-bottom of the screen. No aiming required — the whole lower third of the screen is the bell.

**Why it might work:** Snap is pure *recognition under mild time pressure*, which is the memory function most preserved in early-to-moderate dementia. There is no naming, no recall, no verbal demand. Crucially, the deck itself is doing the remembering — the patient only has to notice sameness, a perceptual judgement. Repeated exposure to the same faces across sessions gives incidental priming for later naming tasks. The bell-slap is a whole-arm gesture that feels good with tremor rather than in spite of it.

**Failure mode:** Speed is the danger. If the deal rate is calibrated for a normal adult, the patient hits the bell three cards late every time and experiences their own slowness explicitly. Also, an over-eager patient who slaps constantly must never be told they were wrong — the wrong slap must simply produce a soft, warm "not that one" chime, never a buzzer. And a patient who never slaps must still see the round end in a satisfying way.

**Difficulty to build:** low

**Variant of:** novel

## 2. Pairs That Stay Turned

**One line:** Twelve cards face-down on the table; the patient turns over any two looking for a matching pair, and any card they've already turned stays face-up for the rest of the game so the board gets easier and easier as they go.

**Input needed:** 6+ photos that can be duplicated as pairs — or better, 6 pairs of *related* images (a person's face + that person's handwriting; a house + the street sign; a wedding photo + the wedding invitation).

**Interaction:** Tap a card. Cards are large — six per screen maximum, each the size of a real playing card held at arm's length. Turn animation with a paper sound.

**Why it might work:** Classic Pelmanism is a working-memory test and therefore cruel. The inversion — cards *stay revealed* once seen — converts it into an **errorless learning** task with a guaranteed win condition. Time spent looking at the board is time spent looking at their own life. The difficulty curve runs *downward*, so the patient's subjective experience is "I'm getting better at this as I go", which is the exact opposite of their experience of most of their day.

**Failure mode:** A patient who cannot form the link between the paired items (face + handwriting) may feel the pairing is arbitrary and become confused about what is being asked. Also, if the board is too large the sheer visual density is overwhelming — a 6x4 grid reads as a wall of noise to poor vision.

**Difficulty to build:** low

**Variant of:** Family Snap

## 3. Dominoes of Days

**One line:** The patient is dealt a hand of chunky dominoes where each half is a photo instead of a number, and plays one onto the end of the line whenever it shares something — same person, same place, same decade — with the tile already down.

**Input needed:** 20+ photos tagged with at least one shared attribute each (person, place, or era). The family tagging step can be as light as dragging photos into named piles.

**Interaction:** Drag a domino from the hand at the bottom to either end of the line. Generous magnetic snapping — anywhere within a couple of inches of a valid end pulls the tile in with a satisfying clack. Invalid placements slide gently back rather than being rejected.

**Why it might work:** Dominoes is deeply procedural for this generation — the *hand shape* of playing a tile is motor memory, not episodic memory, so many patients can play the game correctly while unable to say what they're doing. It targets **semantic and categorical association** rather than recall: "these two go together" is a judgement that survives much longer than "this is my sister Margaret". And it produces a physical artefact — a growing line of their life across the table — that a visiting relative can be shown.

**Failure mode:** If the "shared attribute" rule is not visually obvious, the patient can't see why a move was legal and feels arbitrary. The bigger risk is running out of playable tiles, which in real dominoes means "knocking" — that must be reframed as a normal, dignified part of the game ("nothing to play — take one from the boneyard"), never as being stuck.

**Difficulty to build:** medium

**Variant of:** Pairs That Stay Turned

## 4. Bingo of a Lifetime

**One line:** The patient gets a bingo card of nine pictures from their own life, a familiar voice calls the items out one at a time, and they dab the square with their finger until the card is full and the room applauds.

**Input needed:** 9-15 photos plus, ideally, a family member recording the "call" for each one in caller's rhythm ("Number four — the caravan at Filey!"). Falls back to text-to-speech if no recordings.

**Interaction:** Tap the square. Huge dab-marker splat appears — wet ink, slightly off-centre, like a real dabber. Nothing else on screen.

**Why it might work:** Bingo is the single most culturally loaded game format for this cohort — the hall, the caller's patter, the sociability. Mechanically it is **cued recognition with the answer already in front of you**, so failure is nearly impossible; the "skill" is only locating the square, a visual search task. The caller's voice adds **social presence**, and the caller being their own son or daughter adds **emotional salience** to what would otherwise be a rote scan. Full house is guaranteed because the caller calls every square eventually — the game *cannot* be lost.

**Failure mode:** Visual search on a 3x3 grid is genuinely hard with macular degeneration, and a patient who can't find the square while the caller waits will feel exposed. Needs a gentle auto-highlight after a long pause, framed as the caller helping ("it's over on the left there, love"), not as a correction. In a care home, a shared-tablet version could accidentally make a slower patient visible to faster ones.

**Difficulty to build:** medium

**Variant of:** novel

## 5. The Wireless

**One line:** The patient turns a big brass tuning dial and the radio drifts through stations — songs from their teens and twenties, a bit of crackle between them, and occasionally their granddaughter's voice reading out a letter — and they can simply sit and listen for as long as they like.

**Input needed:** Birth year and rough locality (to auto-select era music), plus optionally any number of family voice recordings of any length. Genuinely works with zero family input.

**Interaction:** Turn a dial, or nothing at all. Leaving it alone is a complete and valid way to use it.

**Why it might work:** **Purely delightful — no testing component.** Music from ages 15-25 is the most durably encoded autobiographical material a human has, retrievable in advanced dementia when almost nothing else is. This mechanic makes no demand whatsoever, which means it is the safe harbour of the whole app: on a bad day, a frightened or exhausted patient can be steered here and still have a good session. The tuning dial is a **procedural motor pleasure** — the resistance and the crackle between stations are the point. Music also acts as a **primer**: patients often become more verbal and more autobiographical for 10-20 minutes after era music, so this is the ideal opener before anything else.

**Failure mode:** Music is a blunt emotional instrument. A specific song can trigger grief with no warning — a first dance, a funeral hymn — and the patient may be unable to explain why they're crying. Needs a one-touch way for a relative to blacklist tracks, and needs to never, ever autoplay something unvetted at high emotional risk (wedding songs, wartime songs, hymns) without family sign-off.

**Difficulty to build:** medium

**Variant of:** novel

## 6. The Slide Carousel

**One line:** The patient presses a big lever, the projector goes *ka-chunk*, and the next family photo appears on the screen with a relative's recorded voice saying who's in it and what was happening — over and over, for as long as they want.

**Input needed:** Any number of photos (works from 5). Optionally a 10-20 second voice caption per photo recorded by a family member.

**Interaction:** Press one large lever/button. That is the entire interface. Auto-advances if untouched.

**Why it might work:** **Purely delightful — no testing component.** This is the slide-night ritual almost every one of these patients hosted or endured in the 1960s and 70s, and the *ka-chunk* is a sound their body knows. Because the voice-over supplies the names and the story, this is textbook **errorless learning** — the patient is never asked, only told, so repeated exposure builds familiarity without ever creating an error trace. The relative's voice provides **social presence** at 3pm on a Tuesday when nobody is visiting. The lever gives agency: they control the pace, which is a rare experience for someone whose day is scheduled by others.

**Failure mode:** Photos of deceased people spoken about in the present tense can cause acute distress or repeated re-grieving. Also, a patient may believe the person is in the next room. Voice captions recorded carelessly ("Do you remember this, Mum?") turn a delight into a test and must be caught in the family-side authoring flow.

**Difficulty to build:** low

**Variant of:** novel

## 7. Shove-Ha'penny Memory Lane

**One line:** The patient flicks a coin with their finger and it slides up a wooden board, and whichever bed it lands in opens up and plays a memory — a photo, a song, a voice — with no right or wrong place to land.

**Input needed:** 5-9 pieces of any content (photos, clips, voice notes). No tagging, no structure.

**Interaction:** Drag-and-release flick. Physics: the coin has weight and friction and the flick strength maps to distance. Tremor produces a wobbly, funny shot, which is fine.

**Why it might work:** **No testing component.** This is a **procedural motor** game — pub skill, entirely in the hands, and hand skill is the last thing to go. The randomness means the patient is never choosing content, so they can never choose "wrong" or reveal that they don't recognise anything. The pleasure loop is the flick itself; the memory is the reward for a good shot, which inverts the usual structure where the memory is the demand. Also generates genuine **anticipation** — watching a coin decelerate is a small suspense engine.

**Failure mode:** Requires enough motor control for a directional flick; severe arthritis or a very weak grip makes the coin barely move, which is a visible physical failure rather than a cognitive one — arguably worse. Needs an assist mode where any touch produces a decent shot. On a slippery tablet in a care home, the coin can feel unresponsive and read as "the game is broken", which patients often internalise as "I've broken it".

**Difficulty to build:** medium

**Variant of:** The Slide Carousel

## 8. Four-Piece Jigsaw

**One line:** A family photograph lies on the table in four big chunky pieces and the patient slides them together, with the pieces jumping into place when they get close, until the picture is whole and the person in it says hello.

**Input needed:** 1+ photo with a clear subject (a face, a house, a dog). Optionally a voice greeting to play on completion.

**Interaction:** Drag with a whole finger or palm. Very generous snapping — within a couple of centimetres the piece leaps home with a wooden clunk. Pieces cannot be placed wrongly; they only go where they go.

**Why it might work:** No testing component in any meaningful sense — the task is **visuospatial and procedural**, and the snapping makes it **structurally unfailable**. Jigsaws are a lifelong Sunday-afternoon habit for many in this cohort, so the activity itself carries positive affective load before any content appears. The gradual reveal creates **anticipation and pattern-completion pleasure**, and the completion of a face triggers strong recognition responses even in patients who cannot name the face. Four pieces is deliberately trivially easy; the number should scale on a per-patient basis and *quietly* reduce if a session goes badly, never with an announcement.

**Failure mode:** If the piece count is set too high by a well-meaning family member ("Mum was brilliant at jigsaws"), the patient hits a wall on an activity they have a lifetime of pride in, which is uniquely humiliating. Piece count must not be family-configurable upward without a warning, or should be system-adaptive only.

**Difficulty to build:** low

**Variant of:** novel

## 9. Quiz Night: Before or After

**One line:** A jingle plays, a photo comes up on the big screen with a question in the host's voice — "Now then: was this before or after you and Bill were married?" — and the patient presses one of two big buttons, and either way there's a story afterwards.

**Input needed:** 6+ photos with approximate years, plus 2-3 anchor life events with years (marriage, a move, a child's birth) supplied by the family.

**Interaction:** Two enormous buttons, side by side, filling the bottom half of the screen. Red and blue like a game show buzzer, labelled in large words AND spoken aloud.

**Why it might work:** Binary forced choice means a 50% floor — **luck protects dignity**, and a wrong answer is a coin-flip, not a symptom. Temporal ordering against a strong personal anchor ("before I was married") is a well-preserved form of **autobiographical retrieval** that works even when exact dates are gone, because it relies on schema rather than episode. The quiz-show framing (jingle, host patter, applause) is the crucial disguise: this generation watched *Sale of the Century* and *Mr and Mrs* and expects to get quiz questions wrong on television without shame. Getting it wrong on a quiz show is *part of the fun*.

**Failure mode:** The host's tone is everything — one wrong inflection and it becomes a doctor's cognitive screen with a jingle on top. A "wrong" answer must be met with a story, not a correction ("Ah, it was just after — that was the summer you moved to Kendal"), and it must never accumulate a visible score. Also risky if the anchor event is emotionally live (a deceased spouse) or if the patient has forgotten they were married, in which case the question itself is a catastrophic reveal.

**Difficulty to build:** medium

**Variant of:** novel

## 10. What's It Worth?

**One line:** An object from the patient's own house appears on a velvet cloth — the carriage clock, the Toby jug, Dad's cigarette case — the patient has a guess at what an expert would say it's worth, and then the "expert" reveals a value *and* their own family's story about the object.

**Input needed:** 5+ photos of personal objects from the home, ideally shot against a plain background, plus a one-sentence family note per object. A rough value is optional and can be invented generously.

**Interaction:** Slide a big chunky lever along a value scale, or pick one of three price cards. Then tap to reveal.

**Why it might work:** *Antiques Roadshow* is appointment television for this demographic and the format is built entirely on **anticipation and reveal** with no penalty for a wrong guess — nobody has ever felt stupid guessing low on a vase. Because valuation is genuinely a matter of luck and expertise, the patient's guess carries zero diagnostic signal, which makes it fully dignity-safe. The real payload is the reveal: **emotionally salient object cueing** is powerful for autobiographical retrieval, and household objects are often better cues than photographs because they carry tactile and use memory. Objects from their own home also anchor the patient in place, which can reduce "wanting to go home" agitation.

**Failure mode:** An object the patient doesn't recognise as theirs, or which triggers a belief that it's been taken/stolen — a common paranoid theme in dementia — can escalate quickly. Also, valuing possessions can prompt distressing thoughts about wills, dying, and things being sold off after their death.

**Difficulty to build:** medium

**Variant of:** Quiz Night: Before or After

## 11. Complete the Saying

**One line:** A voice starts a saying the patient has known all their life — "Many hands..." — and pauses, waiting, and the patient says the ending out loud, and the voice finishes it with them.

**Input needed:** Nothing for the standard proverbs. Family adds value by supplying 5-10 **family-specific** catchphrases and sayings ("What did Grandad always say when it rained?") and, ideally, the original speaker's recorded voice.

**Interaction:** Speak aloud. No input required and none detected — the app simply waits a beat and then completes the phrase together with them. Optionally a "say it again" button.

**Why it might work:** Overlearned verbal sequences live in **semantic and procedural memory** and survive remarkably late — patients who cannot name their own children will complete a proverb reflexively. This makes it near-guaranteed success, delivered as a *game* rather than an exercise. Because nothing is scored or even detected, the patient cannot fail; if they say nothing, the phrase completes anyway and reads as a sing-along rather than a missed answer. Family catchphrases in the original speaker's voice add enormous **emotional salience** and give the family a genuinely joyful authoring task. This is also the classic warm-up: getting a verbal response out early makes later verbal engagement more likely (**priming**).

**Failure mode:** Very low risk, but a patient with anomia or aphasia who *knows* the ending and can't produce it experiences an acutely frustrating tip-of-the-tongue state, made worse by the pause. The waiting beat must be short and forgiving. A deceased person's voice saying their catchphrase can be either the loveliest or the most devastating thing in the app, depending entirely on the day.

**Difficulty to build:** low

**Variant of:** novel

## 12. The Bouncing Ball Sing-Along

**One line:** A song they've known since they were nineteen plays with the words on screen and a white ball bouncing along them, and at the end of each line the ball hangs in the air for a beat and lets the patient sing the rhyming word themselves.

**Input needed:** Nothing — derived from era/locality. Enhanced enormously if a family member records themselves singing along, so it becomes a duet with their daughter.

**Interaction:** Sing aloud, or hum, or tap along, or just watch. All are complete.

**Why it might work:** Musical memory is stored separately from declarative memory and is famously spared. The bouncing-ball sing-along is a genuine cinema-and-Palladium format from this generation's youth, so the *convention* is familiar and needs no instruction. Withholding the rhyme word is a **cued-recall task disguised as a sing-along**, and rhyme + melody + rhythm together form the strongest triple cue available — success rates are very high, so it functions as errorless practice. Singing also engages breath and voice, which lifts mood measurably, and if a relative's voice is on the track, it is a **shared activity with someone who isn't there**.

**Failure mode:** Being unable to keep up with lyrics scrolling is a visible, real-time failure. Also, a patient who was never a singer may find being invited to sing embarrassing, especially on a shared tablet in a communal lounge where others can hear. Needs to work perfectly as a pure listen-and-watch experience with no implied obligation.

**Difficulty to build:** medium

**Variant of:** The Wireless

## 13. Who's on the Line?

**One line:** A telephone rings — a real bell, not a mobile ringtone — the patient picks up the handset, and a familiar voice says "Hello love, it's me" and chats for thirty seconds, and the patient talks back.

**Input needed:** 3+ recorded voice messages of 20-40 seconds each from different family members, recorded as if speaking on the phone. Family should be coached to leave natural gaps for the patient to reply into.

**Interaction:** Lift a handset off a cradle — a big drag or a tap on a very obvious ringing phone. Then speak.

**Why it might work:** The telephone is an interaction schema so deeply overlearned that it requires zero instruction — this cohort has answered ringing phones for seventy years and the **procedural script** ("Hello? Oh hello!") fires automatically. It delivers **social presence** in a form the patient's brain accepts as real connection, which addresses loneliness rather than cognition. Voice-only also strips away the confusion of video calls, where patients often can't work out where the person is. Recognising a voice is **recognition, not recall**, and the caller identifies themselves anyway, so it's errorless.

**Failure mode:** Serious risk of the patient believing the person is genuinely on the line and then becoming distressed when the call ends, or reporting to staff that their son rang and nobody visited. With a deceased relative's voice this could be severe. Also raises a real ethical question about simulated presence and deception that a clinical board will need to rule on — which is exactly why it belongs in a divergent list rather than being cut now.

**Difficulty to build:** medium

**Variant of:** novel

## 14. Beetle Drive

**One line:** The patient rolls a big wooden die and each number adds another piece to a picture of a day in their life — roll a three and you get the weather, roll a five and you get who was there — until the whole scene is assembled and someone tells the story of it.

**Input needed:** For each "day": 5-6 content fragments tagged loosely (a place photo, a person photo, a date, a song from that year, an object, a one-line family note).

**Interaction:** Tap or shake to roll. Big die, physics, satisfying tumble and rattle. Pieces fly in and assemble themselves.

**Why it might work:** Beetle drives were a village-hall staple and the format is pure luck — **there is no skill component at all**, so there is literally nothing that can be revealed about the patient's cognition. What the mechanic actually does is **scaffold autobiographical retrieval piece by piece**: rather than asking "tell me about your wedding day" (a catastrophic open recall demand), it supplies the weather, then the church, then the dress, then a face, and each fragment cues the next. Reconstructive retrieval with incremental cues is far more productive than free recall, and here the cues arrive disguised as dice luck. Also good with a relative sitting alongside taking turns.

**Failure mode:** Slow. A patient with poor attention may lose the thread of what is being assembled before it completes, ending in incoherence rather than a story. If the die is genuinely random the same fragment can be rolled repeatedly, which reads as the game being broken or the patient having done something wrong.

**Difficulty to build:** medium

**Variant of:** novel

## 15. The Tombola

**One line:** The patient turns a handle, a wire drum full of raffle tickets spins and rattles, a ticket drops out, and whatever's written on it is what they get — a song, a photo, a voice, a joke — a lucky dip of their own life.

**Input needed:** Nothing new — draws from all existing content. Works as the app's default "surprise me" surface.

**Interaction:** Turn a crank handle in a circle, or one big tap. Rattling drum, ticket flutters down.

**Why it might work:** This is the **luck-protects-dignity principle in its purest form** and doubles as the app's navigation model for a patient who cannot use menus, tabs or hamburgers — you don't choose, you draw. Choice paralysis is a genuine and under-discussed problem for this population; a fete tombola is a familiar, joyful mechanism for having the choice made for you. The rattle-and-drop creates real **anticipation** out of content the patient has already seen many times, which is how it stays fresh in a daily-use product. And it can silently be weighted by the system toward content that's due for spaced repetition, so the clinical scheduling is completely invisible to the patient.

**Failure mode:** Very little — but if a draw lands on emotionally heavy content when the patient is fragile, the randomness that protects dignity also removes the safety rail. Needs mood-aware weighting or a family-set "not today" list. Repeated draws of the same ticket read as bad luck, which is fine, or as broken, which is not.

**Difficulty to build:** low

**Variant of:** Beetle Drive

## 16. The Vanishing Name

**One line:** A photo of a grandchild appears with their name printed large underneath and the patient reads it out loud; over the following days the name gets fainter and fainter until one day the patient is saying it before it's even appeared, and it never comes back.

**Input needed:** 3-6 photos of key people the family most wants remembered, plus name and relationship. One photo per person is enough.

**Interaction:** Read aloud, then tap the photo to hear the name said back in a family member's voice. Or just tap and listen.

**Why it might work:** This is **spaced retrieval with vanishing cues** — one of the very few interventions with a real evidence base for face-name learning in mild-to-moderate dementia — dressed up as a magic trick rather than a drill. Because the cue starts fully present and fades only when the patient is succeeding, it is **errorless learning**: the patient essentially never experiences not knowing. The fade is imperceptible day to day, so the subjective experience is not "the app is taking my crutch away" but "I know this one". If performance drops, the name simply comes back to full strength with no comment, which means a bad week is invisible.

**Failure mode:** The most testing-flavoured mechanic here, and the one most at risk of feeling like flashcards at the doctor's. If the fade outpaces learning, the patient watches themselves fail on their own grandchild's name, which is close to the worst experience the app could produce. The adaptivity must be conservative and asymmetric — fade slowly, restore instantly.

**Difficulty to build:** medium

**Variant of:** novel

## 17. Two Truths and a Tall Tale

**One line:** The patient's son tells them three things about the family — two that really happened and one that's completely daft — and the patient picks the daft one and everyone has a laugh about it.

**Input needed:** Sets of three short recorded statements per round, two true and one deliberately, comically false, recorded by a family member. Family authoring flow must push hard toward *absurd* rather than *plausible* falsehoods.

**Interaction:** Tap one of three large cards, each of which speaks when touched so they can be replayed freely.

**Why it might work:** The task is **plausibility judgement against personal semantic knowledge**, which holds up far better than episodic recall — a patient may not remember the holiday but still knows perfectly well that Grandad never wrestled a bear. Because the false item is designed to be ridiculous, the hit rate is very high and the failure is funny rather than diagnostic. Humour is doing structural work here: laughing is incompatible with feeling tested. It also gives family members something genuinely enjoyable to author, which matters a great deal for content-supply sustainability.

**Failure mode:** If families write plausible falsehoods (they will, unless prevented), it becomes a memory test with a 33% floor and confabulation risk — worse, the patient may *incorporate* the false statement into their life story, which is an active harm. Also, a patient who picks a true statement as the lie is effectively being told their own memory is wrong, which must be handled as "ah, that one's true actually — and here's the story", never as a correction.

**Difficulty to build:** low

**Variant of:** Quiz Night: Before or After

## 18. The Postcard Rack

**One line:** A spinning rack of postcards of places — some the patient lived, worked or holidayed in, some they've never seen — and they pull each one off and drop it into one of two boxes: "Been there" or "Fancy a visit".

**Input needed:** 4+ place names the patient has a connection to (family supplies place names only; imagery can be sourced from stock/archive photography of the right era). Optional family note per place.

**Interaction:** Drag a card from the rack into one of two large open boxes. Both boxes are equally attractive; neither is the "right" one.

**Why it might work:** The genius is that **there is no wrong answer by construction** — "fancy a visit" is a perfectly dignified thing to say about the town you were born in, so a recognition failure is indistinguishable from a preference. The researcher still gets clean recognition data; the patient experiences a holiday-planning daydream. Place recognition draws on **spatial and semantic memory** with strong emotional colouring, and period-correct imagery (a 1950s seafront rather than today's) hits **era-congruent cueing** much harder. The sorting-into-boxes gesture is itself calming and familiar — it's sorting the post.

**Failure mode:** Places connected to loss (a home that was sold, a town where a spouse died) can be distressing without warning. Sorting the place you currently live into "fancy a visit" is a moment a carer watching over the shoulder may find upsetting even if the patient doesn't — the mechanic needs to protect the *family's* feelings too. Also risks fuelling a desire to travel or "go home" that cannot be met.

**Difficulty to build:** low

**Variant of:** Dominoes of Days

## 19. The Window Seat

**One line:** A window onto a garden that matches the real season and time of day, where family photos occasionally drift past on the breeze like leaves, birds come and go, and rain runs down the glass — and the patient just watches, or taps the glass to make a bird come closer.

**Input needed:** Nothing — works with zero content. Any photos present get woven into the scene automatically.

**Interaction:** None required. Optional light tapping on the glass.

**Why it might work:** **No testing component.** This is the app's idle state, sundowning surface, and off-ramp: something that is still *on* and still *theirs* when the session is over or when the patient is too agitated, tired or anxious for anything with a demand in it. Season/time congruence provides gentle **temporal orientation** without ever stating a date, which is dignity-preserving compared to a big "TODAY IS TUESDAY" reminder board. Ambient nature imagery has an evidence base for agitation reduction in dementia care settings. Photos drifting past deliver **incidental exposure** with zero obligation — a face may be looked at forty times a week without ever being asked about.

**Failure mode:** Could be mistaken for a real window, which is disorienting, or could increase distress in a patient who wants to go outside and can't. On a shared care-home tablet it might be seen by staff as "the app isn't doing anything", and get switched off. Drifting photos of the deceased with no context or warning is a small but real risk.

**Difficulty to build:** medium

**Variant of:** The Slide Carousel

## 20. A Hand With Someone

**One line:** The patient sits down to a game of cards or dominoes where the other player is their daughter, forty miles away, whose face is in a small window at the top of the table and whose tiles appear as she plays them.

**Input needed:** A willing family member with a phone, plus whatever content feeds the underlying game. Nothing else.

**Interaction:** The same interaction as whichever base game is being played (drag a domino, tap a card), plus talking.

**Why it might work:** For most of this cohort, games were never solitary — a game was a *reason to sit with someone*. This reframes every other mechanic in the app from "exercise" to "visit", which is a categorical change in how it will be experienced and in whether it gets used daily. **Social presence** is real rather than simulated, sidestepping the ethical problem in *Who's on the Line?*. The game gives the conversation a structure, which massively helps relatives who find open-ended visits with a parent who doesn't recognise them agonising — a shared task is far easier than a shared silence. Turn-taking is procedural and needs no explanation.

**Failure mode:** The remote relative can drift into testing ("Do you remember who that is, Mum?") within ninety seconds — the format actively invites it and would need coaching or even in-game nudges to prevent. Video of a face can be confusing if the patient can't work out where the person is. And the mechanic's biggest failure mode is availability: a patient who gets used to a Tuesday game and then doesn't get one experiences an absence they wouldn't otherwise have felt.

**Difficulty to build:** high

**Variant of:** Dominoes of Days

## Cross-cutting notes for the culling board

- **Session shape.** These are not equivalent units. A good session probably runs *warm-up → play → wind-down*: The Wireless or Complete the Saying to prime, one or two game mechanics, then The Window Seat or The Slide Carousel to land softly. Cull with the shape in mind, not just mechanic by mechanic.
- **The tombola is also the navigation.** If menus are off the table for this user, mechanic 15 is doing double duty as the app's information architecture. Cutting it means solving navigation another way.
- **Luck taxonomy.** Mechanics 9, 10, 14, 15, 17 all carry an explicit chance element specifically to give the patient deniability. If the board strips the luck out to make the data cleaner, it will strip the dignity out with it.
- **What the family will actually record.** Ranked by how likely a busy relative is to supply it: place names and years (trivial) → photos (easy, they have thousands) → one-line notes (fine) → voice captions (needs a good authoring flow) → recorded bingo calls, three-statement rounds, sung duets (needs the flow to be genuinely fun for the family, which is its own design problem).
