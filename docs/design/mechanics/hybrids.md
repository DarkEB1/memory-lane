# Hybrids, Collapses and Missing Siblings

**Lens: hybrid.** Not a new point of view — a pass over the seven boards looking for (a) pairs and triples that are better fused than separate, (b) mechanics that are secretly the same idea wearing different clothes, and (c) obvious siblings nobody generated.

---

## Part 1 — Secretly the same idea (collapse candidates)

These are not new mechanics; they are the map the culling board will need. Each cluster is **one mechanic with several skins**. Build the shell once, vary the dressing.

| Cluster | The one underlying mechanic | Skins generated |
|---|---|---|
| **Two-alternative forced choice** | Recognition probe with two large targets | This One or This One (scientist), It Doesn't Matter Which (reminiscence), Two Doors (ambient), Two Doors (OT — but *spatial*, different content domain), Her Voice Asks (audio), Whose Voice Is That? (audio), Two Good Things (reminiscence), Quiz Night: Before or After (game) |
| **Sentence/lyric completion** | High-constraint cued recall that completes itself | Finish The Sentence, Complete the Saying, Finish the Line, Fill In the Line, Call and Response, The Bouncing Ball Sing-Along |
| **Blur-to-sharp reveal** | Perceptual closure over a personal image | Coming Into Focus (scientist), The Slow Reveal (ambient) |
| **Name that fades week by week** | Vanishing cues / errorless fading | The Fading Name (scientist), The Vanishing Name (game) |
| **Open-ended narration to a listening mic** | Autobiographical narration, ungraded | Tell Me About This One (×2), Talking Back, The Storyteller's Chair, Sunday Afternoon |
| **Voice from a relative arriving asynchronously** | Social presence delivered as audio mail | The Answering Machine, Message Waiting, The Voice Letterbox, Who's on the Line?, The Telephone, The Ringing Telephone, Someone Is Listening, Send One Back |
| **Ambient photo surface with no demand** | Zero-demand exposure | The Photograph That Waits, Pure Exposure, The Frame That Waits, The Idle Screen Life, Lounge Wall, The Living Room Channel, The Window Seat |
| **Period radio** | Era music + familiar voice, no interface | The Wireless (game), The Wireless (audio), Their Own Radio Station (ambient) |

**Consequence for the build:** roughly eight shells cover something like sixty of the generated mechanics. Everything below is written as a *hybrid of two or three of those shells*, not as a sixty-first thing.

## Part 2 — Hybrid and missing mechanics

### 1. The Caller Who Repeats Herself
*Bingo of a Lifetime × The Sentence They Can Keep × The Widening Gap*

- **One line:** She dabs squares on a bingo card made of her own life while the caller keeps coming back, again and again, to the one thing that matters most this month.
- **Input needed:** 9 photos from her life; one family-recorded call per photo in caller's rhythm ("Number four — the caravan at Filey!"); plus the single sentence the family and OT have agreed on for this month ("Jean comes on Wednesdays"), recorded in the same voice.
- **Interaction:** Tap a large square. A wet dabber splat appears. Nothing else on screen.
- **Why it might work:** Spaced retrieval is the best-evidenced technique here but it looks like drilling. Bingo *already* repeats — numbers recur, callers repeat themselves, that is the texture of the game — so a 30s / 1m / 2m / 4m schedule for the target item hides completely inside the format. Recognition, not recall. The card always fills.
- **Failure mode:** If she notices the same square coming up repeatedly and reads it as being tested on the thing they think she can't hold. A card that stalls because she stops dabbing turns a game into an unfinished obligation sitting on the table.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** Bingo of a Lifetime × The Sentence They Can Keep

### 2. Correct the Machine
*Correct Me × Two Truths and a Tall Tale × The Long Introduction*

- **One line:** A cheerful voice gets something about his life wrong — sometimes comically, sometimes only slightly — and he puts it right, and the voice thanks him for setting it straight.
- **Input needed:** 5–10 true facts about him (job, birthplace, wife's name, the car, the regiment), each paired with a family-approved wrong version, written at two strengths: absurd ("you were a deep-sea diver, weren't you?") and near-miss ("you were at Vickers, weren't you?").
- **Interaction:** Speak aloud, or tap one of two large cards that speak when touched.
- **Why it might work:** It inverts error attribution. Errorless learning matters because a patient's error gets encoded implicitly and strengthened — but here the *machine* owns the error, and the patient's utterance is always the correct one. It elicits genuine effortful semantic-autobiographical retrieval while the only thing on the record as wrong is the app. Being the authority in the room is motivationally protective in a way encouragement never is.
- **Failure mode:** If the near-miss is plausible and he accepts it, the app has just asserted a false fact about his own life — and if it then corrects him, that is precisely the humiliation the whole product exists to avoid. Absurd variants can also land as mockery if the joke seems aimed at him rather than at the machine.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** Correct Me × Two Truths and a Tall Tale

### 3. The Helpful Telephone
*Who's on the Line? × The Ladder of Hints × The Answering Machine*

- **One line:** The phone by his chair rings with a real bell, he picks up, and his son chats to him — and when he goes quiet, his son fills the gap himself the way people do on the phone ("...you know, Margaret, the one with the loud laugh").
- **Input needed:** Call-shaped audio from each relative, 45–90 seconds, scripted with the hint ladder built into the conversation: a question, a pause, a soft cue, a longer pause, then the answer said warmly as if just remembering it aloud. Several variants per person so it is never identical.
- **Interaction:** Lift a handset off a cradle, speak, put it down.
- **Why it might work:** Telephone turn-taking is over-learned procedural social behaviour that survives long after the ability to operate a screen. The hint ladder stops being a hint and becomes conversational repair — cueing that never announces itself as cueing. And the phone has no interface to fail at.
- **Failure mode:** A call from someone who has died. Or the discovery mid-call that his son is not actually there — that is a small bereavement, and it can happen every time. Shared handsets in a care home are also an infection-control conversation nobody wants to have.
- **Difficulty:** high
- **Variant of:** Who's on the Line? × The Ladder of Hints

### 4. The Song Opens the Door
*The Song First × The Slow Reveal × Beetle Drive*

- **One line:** Fifteen seconds of a song from when she was nineteen plays before anything appears, and only then does a photograph from that same year swim slowly up out of the blur.
- **Input needed:** Year of birth; 5–10 songs the family confirms mattered; photos tagged loosely by decade. No captions required.
- **Interaction:** Just watch. Optional single tap to hold on a picture.
- **Why it might work:** Music from the reminiscence bump is both a mood regulator and an unusually strong retrieval key — it reinstates the encoding context before the image arrives, so the photograph lands in a prepared mind rather than a cold one. Priming plus perceptual closure, and the picture resolves either way, so it cannot be failed.
- **Failure mode:** A song attached to a loss arriving with nobody in the room. Or the picture resolves into something she does not know at all, after the music has promised her that she will.
- **Difficulty:** low
- **Variant of:** The Song First × The Slow Reveal

### 5. Ask Grandad
*Teach the Grandchild × Can You Help Me With This One? × The Grandchild's Errand*

- **One line:** His granddaughter's voice asks him something the family genuinely does not know the answer to — who the woman in the hat is, what year the boat photo was — and he tells her.
- **Input needed:** 3–5 photos the family has flagged as genuinely unidentified; a 15–30 second recorded question from a named younger relative, phrased as a real request; a photo of that relative.
- **Interaction:** Watch and listen, then speak aloud. No buttons.
- **Why it might work:** Role reversal. Every other mechanic positions him as the one who might not know; this one positions him as the only person alive who does. Remote autobiographical and semantic memory is the most preserved territory, and a question with no known answer is structurally unfailable — there is nothing to be caught out against.
- **Failure mode:** The stakes are now real. If he cannot answer something his family actually needs, the shortfall is consequential and witnessed, which is the exact opposite of a no-stakes probe. Requires a graceful "we'll ask you another day" and a family coached hard never to show disappointment.
- **Difficulty:** low
- **Variant of:** Teach the Grandchild × Can You Help Me With This One?

### 6. The Placemat
*Somebody Sat Down × The Kitchen Table × Table of Objects*

- **One line:** She sits down in her usual chair in the lounge and the tablet lying flat on the table in front of her is already showing her own life — nobody asked her who she was.
- **Input needed:** Nothing new in content terms; per-photo consent flags for communal display; one physical tagged placemat, coaster or chair tag per resident.
- **Interaction:** Sit down. That is all. Optionally put a finger on a photograph lying face-up on the table.
- **Why it might work:** Every shared-device design has a hidden orientation test at the front door — "which one is you?", "tap your name" — and that test is the hardest question in the room. Place-based identification removes it entirely. The flat table also means no screen faces any one person, so two residents can share it without one of them being the audience.
- **Failure mode:** Sitting in the wrong chair and being shown a stranger's family — or, far worse, another resident's late spouse. And the correction ("that's not your seat, love") is a public one.
- **Difficulty:** high
- **Variant of:** Somebody Sat Down × The Kitchen Table

### 7. The Duet
*A Hand With Someone × The Bouncing Ball Sing-Along × Hum Along*

- **One line:** She sings a song she has known since she was nineteen with her daughter, who is forty miles away in a small window at the top of the screen, and the band quietly covers whichever line either of them drops.
- **Input needed:** A willing family member with a phone and ten scheduled minutes. Song list optional — derivable from birth year.
- **Interaction:** Sing, hum, tap a foot, or just watch. All complete.
- **Why it might work:** Singing survives when conversation has gone — musical and procedural memory route around the damaged declarative system entirely. Synchrony delivers social presence without requiring the language production that is where failure usually becomes visible to both of them. No question is asked of anybody.
- **Failure mode:** Network latency makes the duet ragged and she concludes she is the one who cannot keep time. And the daughter, on camera, hearing how her mother sounds now — this mechanic can hurt the family member more than the patient.
- **Difficulty:** high
- **Variant of:** A Hand With Someone × The Bouncing Ball Sing-Along

### 8. Your Own Voice, Waiting
*Pocket Answers × The Legacy Recording × The Handover*

- **One line:** The voice telling him his daughter's name, and where he grew up, and what he did for a living, is his own — recorded by him four years ago, when he could.
- **Input needed:** A recording programme run at or near diagnosis: 20–40 short clips of the patient saying their own facts, names, stories and phrases, with a relative prompting off-camera. Nothing else afterwards.
- **Interaction:** Just watch and listen. Optionally read or say it along with himself.
- **Why it might work:** Self-referential material is encoded and retained preferentially, and own-voice audio is exceptionally salient. It also dissolves the authority problem: being told a fact about yourself by an app or a relative carries a status cost; hearing yourself state it does not. It is not a test and it is not correction — it is testimony.
- **Failure mode:** Hearing your own fluent voice from before, and hearing the distance. That is a mirror, and mirrors are dangerous here. It also advertises absence — families who did not start early enough are shown exactly what they failed to capture.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** Pocket Answers × The Legacy Recording

### 9. The Thermostat
*The Quiet Stopwatch × Fade To Rest × Back Along The Years*

- **One line:** Nothing — she does the session exactly as always and never meets this at all; underneath, how long she takes and how her voice sounds slides every question up and down the demand gradient, walking back into the deeper past whenever the present gets hard.
- **Input needed:** Nothing — derived entirely from response latency, prosody and touch behaviour already captured.
- **Interaction:** No input at all.
- **Why it might work:** It makes the two governing gradients continuous rather than stepped: retrieval demand (watch → recognise → cued recall) and the temporal gradient (recent → remote). Errorless learning only works if you stay inside the band where recognition still succeeds, and that band moves hour to hour with fatigue. A thermostat is the only honest way to track it.
- **Failure mode:** Over-eagerness. Dropping to zero demand on a good day is patronising, and she will notice the day got easier. Slow speech from hearing loss or a dry mouth reads as impairment and strips demand from someone who wanted it. Silent adaptation is also unauditable — the researcher sees drift they cannot attribute to disease or to the app.
- **Difficulty:** high
- **Variant of:** The Quiet Stopwatch × Fade To Rest

### 10. The Twenty Minutes
*Sunday Afternoon × The Living Room Channel × Nothing Today*

- **One line:** Her husband presses one button and the room fills with her own photographs and her own music for twenty minutes, and at the end a voice says "he'll be back in a minute" — and he is.
- **Input needed:** Nothing beyond existing content. The carer sets the length once.
- **Interaction:** No input at all from the patient. One press by the carer.
- **Why it might work:** The determinant of whether this app is still in use in month four is not the patient's engagement, it is carer burden. Exposure-only content is a legitimate outcome by our own rules, so twenty minutes of zero-demand reminiscence is clinically defensible *and* buys a shower. The announced ending prevents the carer returning to an agitated handover.
- **Failure mode:** Being parked. Patients know when they are being managed, and dignity does not survive that recognition. If it ends while he is still out of the room, the silence afterwards is worse than never starting.
- **Difficulty:** low
- **Variant of:** Sunday Afternoon × The Living Room Channel

### 11. The Stone That Answers
*The Standing Answer × The Keepsake That Speaks × Just The One Word*

- **One line:** A smooth wooden pebble sits on the arm of her chair, and when she picks it up a familiar voice tells her the thing she has been asking about — as warmly the thirtieth time as the first.
- **Input needed:** 3–6 short clips per recurring question, recorded by a named relative under the family's disclosure policy: where am I, who is coming, where is he, you're alright. A physical object chosen by the family, ideally one already meaningful.
- **Interaction:** Pick it up and hold it. Put it down to stop. No screen, no button, no target.
- **Why it might work:** Repetitive questioning is anxiety-driven, not information-driven, and the reason it escalates is that the human answer visibly degrades by the tenth repetition. An answer that never tires removes the interpersonal cost. The object's weight and texture is a tactile cue requiring no aiming, no vision and no learned gesture — it works with tremor and with advanced impairment.
- **Failure mode:** The question is "where's Margaret?" and Margaret died in 1998. Every possible answer is either a lie repeated thirty times a day or a bereavement repeated thirty times a day. The object being lost, or picked up by another resident, is its own crisis.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** The Standing Answer × The Keepsake That Speaks

### 12. Two Walks
*Walk Down Your Street × The Way to the Bathroom × Same Town, Same Time*

- **One line:** He taps one big arrow and moves forward one step at a time — and some days that is down the street he grew up in, and some days it is from his chair to his own bathroom door.
- **Input needed:** One address or place name from before roughly age 25 (imagery derived from street-level and archive sources); plus one 15–30 second phone video per real route in his current home, walked at his seated eye height.
- **Interaction:** Tap one large arrow to move one step. Speak aloud if he wants.
- **Why it might work:** One over-learned interaction serving two jobs — remote autobiographical retrieval and functional route learning — so the second borrows the motivational energy of the first. First-person, eye-height footage is what makes route learning actually transfer to walking; a plan view does not.
- **Failure mode:** The two walks blur, and he goes looking for a 1948 front door down a care-home corridor. Street imagery also has no editorial control: a demolished terrace, a boarded-up shop, the house sold when his mother died.
- **Difficulty:** high
- **Variant of:** Walk Down Your Street × The Way to the Bathroom

### 13. Both Are Right
*It Doesn't Matter Which × The Postcard Rack × Odd One In*

- **One line:** Two pictures, and a question with a real preference inside it — "which of these would you rather talk about today?" — and whichever she touches, the app is pleased.
- **Input needed:** 6+ photos across distinct themes, at least some of them true targets from her life; foils drawn automatically from era-matched stock.
- **Interaction:** Tap one of two large pictures, or drag a card into one of two equally attractive boxes.
- **Why it might work:** It keeps the recognition probe intact as *data* — which picture, how quickly, how often the true item is chosen above chance across weeks — while removing the correctness frame entirely from her experience. The outcome measure lives in the choice distribution, not in a right answer. That is how you satisfy the researcher and the no-score rule at once.
- **Failure mode:** A patient with insight who wants to be tested finds it condescending and says so. And the family, looking at the researcher-facing dashboard, will see "she got it wrong" data about sessions the patient was promised contained no wrong answers — a consent and framing problem, not a UI one.
- **Difficulty:** low
- **Variant of:** It Doesn't Matter Which × The Postcard Rack

### 14. Faces That Speak Clearly
*Someone In The Next Room × Touch the Face, Hear the Voice × Read Me This* — the missing sibling: hearing loss

- **One line:** Her son's face fills the screen, well lit and facing her, saying one short thing slowly, with the same words written huge underneath.
- **Input needed:** 20–40 seconds of video per relative, filmed face-forward in good light, no backlighting, spoken slowly with the mouth visible; plus the exact words typed by the family for captioning.
- **Interaction:** Just watch. Optional touch anywhere to replay.
- **Why it might work:** Age-related hearing loss is the single most common reason a voice mechanic fails, and it is routinely misread — by families, by staff, and by an app measuring response latency — as memory failure. Audiovisual speech is easier to parse than audio alone even for people with intact hearing; lipreading plus captions plus a familiar face restores a channel that half the target population has partially lost.
- **Failure mode:** Reading often goes before listening does, so large text can stack a second failure on top of the first. And a carefully slowed "speaking to a deaf person" delivery is audible as condescension — she can hear that she is being spoken to like a child even when she cannot hear the words.
- **Difficulty:** low
- **Variant of:** Someone In The Next Room × Touch the Face, Hear the Voice

### 15. It Happened, and Here's the Proof
*The Kept Promise × The Thing At Four O'Clock × Yesterday Actually Happened × Someone Is Listening*

- **One line:** In the morning she is told her son is ringing at four; at ten past four she is asked, warmly, whether he rang; and tomorrow morning the first thing she sees is his face and eight seconds of him actually saying goodbye on that call.
- **Input needed:** One real, genuinely-going-to-happen commitment per week from a family member who will keep it; a one-button "I called / I visited" log; optionally 8 seconds of real call audio.
- **Interaction:** Tap one of two large buttons or say yes. The next day, just watch.
- **Why it might work:** Prospective memory with a full external scaffold — cue, probe, and then evidence. The next-day proof is the crucial third act nobody generated: it converts an unverifiable memory claim into a recognition judgement about evidence, which is exactly the operation that repairs the "did that happen, or did I imagine it?" anxiety that drives a great deal of daily distress.
- **Failure mode:** The family does not ring. The app has then announced something, asked her about it, and produced nothing — it has manufactured a disappointment and put it on a schedule. This mechanic is only as kind as the least reliable relative.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** The Kept Promise × The Thing At Four O'Clock

### 16. The Tea That Remembers
*The Thing You Were Already Doing × Tea and Toast × Hands Remember*

- **One line:** He picks up the kettle the way he has forty thousand times, and a small screen on the counter shows his own mug, his own tin — and when the tea is made it shows him the kitchen he had in 1968.
- **Input needed:** 6–10 photos of his actual kitchen, one per step, shot from his standing position; the sequence confirmed by family; a kettle sensor or smart plug; plus 3+ photos of an earlier kitchen from his life.
- **Interaction:** No input at all. Making tea is the interaction.
- **Why it might work:** Procedural memory scaffolded *in situ*, at the moment of use, which is where transfer normally fails — rehearsing a sequence on a tablet in the lounge does not survive the walk to the kitchen. And it ends on reminiscence rather than instruction, so the last beat is a gift and not supervision.
- **Failure mode:** Being watched by your own kitchen. Prompting a step he was already halfway through is a tiny insult delivered daily, and a sensor that misreads the order tells him he has made a mistake he did not make. Also: a screen near a kettle.
- **Difficulty:** high
- **Variant of:** Tea and Toast × The Thing You Were Already Doing

### 17. A Ticket for Somebody Else
*The Tombola × Postbag Between Homes × Can You Help Me With This One?*

- **One line:** She turns the crank, the drum rattles, a ticket flutters out — and this one is not from her life at all, it is a photograph of a shipyard from a man in Glasgow who worked in one like the one her father worked in, and she is asked if she wants to say something back to him.
- **Input needed:** Nothing new from her family — matched across homes on existing place, occupation and era tags. Requires an inter-home consent framework and a moderation step on outgoing replies.
- **Interaction:** Turn a crank, or one big tap. Then speak aloud if she wants, or say nothing.
- **Why it might work:** Luck removes any diagnostic reading — a tombola gives you what it gives you. Shared generational and geographic context means the recognition demand is on *era-general* semantic memory, the most robust store of all, rather than on personal episodic detail. And it makes her a contributor to someone else's day, which is the strongest motivational frame available to us.
- **Failure mode:** A stranger's content can carry anything — a pit disaster, a regiment, a hospital. And a reply that gets sent onward and never answered is a small abandonment. There is also a real risk of her absorbing another person's memory as her own, which the family will find distressing even if she does not.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** The Tombola × Postbag Between Homes

### 18. Snap, With the Same Faces Coming Round
*Family Snap × The Widening Gap × The Return Visit*

- **One line:** Cards flip over on the table and when the same face comes up twice in a row he slaps the big brass bell — and the three faces the family most wants him to keep are the ones the deck keeps quietly dealing him, at wider and wider intervals.
- **Input needed:** 8+ photos, at least two distinct shots per person, 4+ people; family marks 3–5 priority people. Optionally a 2-second cheer or laugh clip per person.
- **Interaction:** One enormous bell-push filling the lower third of the screen. No aiming required.
- **Why it might work:** The expanding-interval schedule is the active ingredient; Snap is the anaesthetic. The deck order is fully under the app's control while looking entirely like shuffle, so the priority faces recur at 20s, 60s, 3m — and a missed bell is a missed card, which is what happens in Snap, not a failed trial. Motor response instead of verbal response also sidesteps word-finding altogether.
- **Failure mode:** A slapped bell on a non-match is still a visible mistake in front of whoever is in the room, and the bell is loud. Tremor produces accidental slaps that make the game feel out of his control. And a face that keeps returning may be read as the app insisting on someone he does not want to see.
- **Difficulty:** medium
- **Variant of:** Family Snap × The Widening Gap

## Part 3 — Siblings still missing after this pass

Named here rather than fully specified, for whoever runs the next divergent round:

- **Touch and texture.** One board did smell (The Smell of It). Nobody did fabric, weight, temperature — a knitted square, a cold doorknob, a hot mug. Tactile cues are cheap, need no screen, and survive very late.
- **Animals.** No mechanic anywhere uses a dog, a cat, birds, a farm. For a great many patients the most emotionally salient non-human content in their life is a dog.
- **The patient's own handwriting.** Referenced once, in passing, as a Pairs card. Reading your own hand is self-referential, procedural and unusually moving.
- **Movement of the whole body.** Hands Remember and Hands That Knew How are both seated and manual. Nothing walks, dances, or stands up.
- **The bad day, in the moment.** Nothing Today handles a bad day chosen in advance. Nothing handles agitation arriving mid-session at 90 seconds in.
- **The family member who has stopped coming.** Every social mechanic assumes a willing relative. Several assume one who replies within 24 hours. Nobody designed for the son who has not visited since March.
