# Mechanics: Orientation & Daily Life

**Lens: The Occupational Therapist**

Divergent generation phase. Nothing here is filtered for feasibility, cost, or clinical
evidence — a later board culls. The bias throughout is toward *functional* memory: the
things that let someone get through Tuesday without fear, rather than abstract recall
scores.

---

## Framing: the OT position

Standard spaced-repetition logic (show item, ask for answer, mark right/wrong) is
actively harmful here. It manufactures failure, and failure in this population is not a
learning signal — it is a humiliation event that ends the session and colours the rest of
the day. Every mechanic below is built on three OT commitments:

1. **Recognition over recall.** Choosing between two large pictures is preserved far
   longer than producing a name from nothing. Where recall is targeted, it is always
   *cued* recall with the cue arriving before the patient can fail.
2. **Errorless learning + spaced retrieval.** The correct answer is shown *first*, then
   asked back at expanding intervals. The patient is never invited to guess. "Don't
   worry about guessing — I'll show you" is the house style.
3. **Function over score.** The outcome that matters is "she was calm when her son
   arrived," not "she named 7/10 faces." Difficulty adapts downward silently and never
   announces that it has done so.

### The repeated-grief problem, and correcting vs. going along

The hardest ethical surface in this whole product. If someone asks daily where their dead
wife is, telling them the truth daily means bereaving them daily — each time as fresh as
the first, because the correction cannot be retained but the emotion can. The literature
splits between *reality orientation* (tell the truth, always), *validation therapy*
(respond to the feeling, not the fact), and *therapeutic lying / therapeutic fibbing*
(a comfortable untruth), with SPECAL-style approaches going furthest toward never
contradicting at all. Nursing ethics writing is genuinely unresolved on this and the
critiques of routine deception (erosion of trust, loss of autonomy, staff habituation to
lying) are serious.

The product cannot pick a side. The design position for every mechanic below:

- **The app never volunteers a death, a diagnosis, or a loss.** Bereavement facts are
  only ever surfaced in response to the patient reaching for them, never as a scheduled
  card, never as a "did you know."
- **The family sets a per-person disclosure policy** during setup, in plain language, with
  three settings and a worked example of what the patient will actually see:
  - *Tell gently* — "Bill died three years ago. You were married fifty-one years."
    Followed immediately by warmth, not silence.
  - *Answer the feeling* — no factual claim either way: "You're missing Bill. He was
    a lovely man. Here he is on the boat at Salcombe."
  - *Go along* — the family writes the sentence themselves. The app never invents a
    comforting lie; a human being must author it and own it.
- **Lead with the emotion, follow with the person, and only then the fact (if enabled).**
  Never fact-first.
- **The clinical team can see the policy and change it.** Care-home deployments must
  respect the home's own policy, not the family's, where they conflict.
- **No mechanic ever corrects a spoken false belief.** If the patient says "I'm going home
  to Mother's after this," the app does not argue. Detected contradictions are logged for
  the family, never rebutted on screen.

## The mechanics

### 1. Who's Coming Today

**One line:** The patient sees a big photo of the one person visiting them today, hears
that person's own voice say "It's me, Sarah, I'm coming at three," and taps the picture to
hear it again as many times as they like.

**Input needed:** A calendar entry from the family (date + person), one clear face photo
of that person taken in the last two years, and a 10-second voice clip recorded by that
person saying their name and when they're coming.

**Interaction:** Tap one large picture (the whole screen is the button). Repeats are free
and unlimited.

**Why it might work:** Priming plus emotional salience. It doesn't rely on the patient
remembering across hours — it lowers the startle and "who are you?" panic at the doorway,
which is the actual functional harm. The relative's real voice recruits social presence
and voice familiarity, which survives long after facial recognition degrades.

**Failure mode:** The visit gets cancelled and the patient waits at the window all
afternoon. Devastating, and it's the app's fault. Needs a hard rule that only confirmed
visits appear, plus a graceful "Sarah is coming another day — here she is at the beach
instead" reroute that never uses the word "cancelled."

**Difficulty:** medium · **Variant of:** novel

---

### 2. The Quiet Anchor

**One line:** Before anything is asked of them, the patient just watches a slow ten-second
card that says what day it is, what the weather is doing outside their actual window, and
what season it is — with a photo of their own street or garden in that season.

**Input needed:** Postcode/location (for weather), and ideally 4 photos of the view from
their own window or front door, one per season. Falls back to generic seasonal imagery.

**Interaction:** No input at all. It plays. There is no question and no button.

**Why it might work:** Orientation without confrontation. Classic reality-orientation
boards work, but *asking* "what day is it?" is the confrontational part that produces
distress. Stripping the question keeps the orientation benefit and deletes the failure
surface. Also functions as a session warm-up that lets attention settle before any demand.

**Failure mode:** If the weather shown contradicts the actual window ("it says sunny, it's
pouring") the patient learns the screen lies, and trust in every other mechanic falls with
it. Also risks feeling institutional and infantilising if it's styled like a nursing-home
whiteboard.

**Difficulty:** low · **Variant of:** novel

---

### 3. Today's Face on the Door

**One line:** The patient taps to see the photo and first name of the carer who is
actually on shift right now, with a one-line note in the carer's own words ("I'm Amina,
I'll bring your tea at ten").

**Input needed:** Care-home rota integration or a two-tap staff check-in; one photo and
one recorded sentence per staff member, refreshed when rotas change.

**Interaction:** Tap one large picture. Optionally the carer taps their own face at the
start of shift, and the patient just watches.

**Why it might work:** The single most anxiety-producing question in residential care is
"who is this person in my room?" Recognition is far easier than recall, and pre-exposure
(priming) minutes before the carer walks in changes the encounter from intrusion to
expected visit. Also dignifies the patient — they get to know staff names, which is
normally a one-way street.

**Failure mode:** Agency staff and unplanned shift swaps make the shown face wrong, which
is worse than showing nothing. In a shared-tablet care home it also risks the patient
believing the person on screen is a family member.

**Difficulty:** high (rota integration) · **Variant of:** Who's Coming Today

---

### 4. The Way to the Bathroom

**One line:** The patient watches a short, silent, first-person video filmed at walking
pace and eye height, going from their own chair to their own bathroom door, and can watch
it again just before they get up.

**Input needed:** A 15–30 second phone video walked by a family member or carer from the
patient's usual chair/bed to the target door, filmed at the patient's seated eye height.
One video per route (bathroom, bedroom, dining room, front door, lounge).

**Interaction:** Just watch. One tap to replay. Optionally a physical "show me the way"
button on a stand near the chair.

**Why it might work:** Route knowledge is largely procedural and visuospatial, and
first-person walkthroughs recruit that rather than declarative memory. It rehearses the
*doing* rather than the *knowing*. Highest-value functional target in the whole set —
continence accidents from not finding the bathroom are a leading cause of dignity loss and
of admission to residential care.

**Failure mode:** The tablet isn't with them when they stand up, so the rehearsal is
useless at the moment of need. Also: filming a route implies the patient can't find their
own bathroom, which is a painful thing to be handed. Framing must be environmental ("the
new building is confusing") not personal.

**Difficulty:** medium · **Variant of:** novel

---

### 5. Two Doors

**One line:** Two large photographs appear side by side — both real doors in the building
the patient is standing in — and they tap the one that's the bathroom.

**Input needed:** Photos of 4–8 real doorways/landmarks in the patient's actual building,
shot from the angle the patient approaches them, plus a label for each.

**Interaction:** Tap one of two large pictures. No timer. No wrong-answer sound.

**Why it might work:** Two-alternative forced choice keeps success rate structurally high
(never below chance, and the distractor can be made maximally different on bad days).
Grounding it in the patient's *real* environment means the learning transfers — generic
icon-matching does not.

**Failure mode:** In a care home, all the doors look identical by design, so the task is
genuinely impossible and the patient concludes they're stupid rather than that the
building is badly designed. Needs distractors chosen for visual distinctiveness, and a
rule that the same door is never wrong twice.

**Difficulty:** low · **Variant of:** The Way to the Bathroom

---

### 6. Yesterday Actually Happened

**One line:** The patient sees a simple picture-and-sentence card: "Yesterday, Margaret
phoned you. You talked about the garden." — with Margaret's photo and, if she left one,
eight seconds of her actually saying goodbye on that call.

**Input needed:** Family taps a one-button "I called / I visited" log after contact, plus
optionally one sentence about what was discussed. Voicemail-style clips are optional. Zero
photos needed beyond the person's existing profile picture.

**Interaction:** Just watch, tap to replay the voice.

**Why it might work:** Attacks the "nobody ever comes to see me" belief — which is
enormously painful for both patient and family and is usually a memory artefact rather
than a fact. Provides external evidence in a way that validates rather than argues.
Emotional salience of the real voice makes it stick further into the day than a written
note would.

**Failure mode:** If a family member logs contact they didn't have, or logs sparsely, the
card becomes an accusation ("nobody has phoned for nine days"). Absence must never be
displayed — only presence. Also risks the patient becoming distressed that they *don't*
remember something the screen insists happened.

**Difficulty:** low · **Variant of:** novel

### 7. The Standing Answer

**One line:** There is always one soft, unlabelled button in the corner with a heart on
it; when the patient presses it — which they might do because they're anxious, or missing
someone, or don't know where they are — they get a warm, pre-recorded answer from someone
who loves them.

**Input needed:** The family records 3–6 short "whenever you need it" clips: one for
loneliness, one for "where am I?", one for "where is [deceased person]?" authored under
the disclosure policy above, one plain "you're alright, we love you." Each written and
spoken by a named relative.

**Interaction:** Tap one large, always-present, unlabelled soft button. No menu, no
choice, no question asked of the patient.

**Why it might work:** Gives the patient *agency over their own reassurance* rather than
making them wait for a human to notice their distress. Because the content is the
relative's real voice, it recruits attachment and social presence. Critically, it inverts
the ethics: the patient reaches for the answer, so the app is responding rather than
volunteering, and the family — not an algorithm — authored every word.

**Failure mode:** Becomes a compulsive loop — pressed forty times an hour, each press
re-triggering fresh grief if the disclosure policy is set to "tell gently." Or it becomes
a substitute for human contact, and staff stop coming. Needs press-frequency monitoring
routed to the family, and a rule that "tell gently" answers are never the first thing
heard on repeated presses within a session.

**Difficulty:** medium · **Variant of:** novel

### 8. Pocket Answers

**One line:** The patient practises, out loud and with the answer already on screen, the
handful of sentences they need when a stranger asks about them — their name, where they
grew up, how many children they have, what they did for a living.

**Input needed:** 5–8 life facts written by the family in the *patient's own phrasing*
("I was a district nurse in Bolton for thirty years"), each with a supporting photo.
Ideally a recording of the patient themselves saying it, from earlier in the illness.

**Interaction:** Speak aloud, reading along with large text and hearing it in their own or
a relative's voice. Speech is not scored, transcribed, or checked.

**Why it might work:** Pure errorless learning plus spaced retrieval on the exact material
that protects dignity in social encounters. Self-produced speech is more durable than
listening. And these are *identity* facts, which carry high autobiographical and emotional
weight — they resist decay better than neutral material.

**Failure mode:** Practising "I have three children" when one has died, or "I live at
Ash Cottage" when they were moved into care last month, embeds a falsehood the patient
will assert confidently and be corrected on by a stranger. Facts must be reviewed by
family on a schedule and stated in the past tense where safer.

**Difficulty:** low · **Variant of:** novel

### 9. The Doorbell Drill

**One line:** A doorbell or knock sounds from the tablet, and a moment later a photo of a
real person from their life appears with their name and relationship spoken aloud — the
patient just says hello, or taps to greet them.

**Input needed:** 8–15 photos of people who realistically turn up at the door, each with a
spoken name-and-relationship line ("It's Tom — your son"). Ideally recorded by that person.

**Interaction:** Watch, then optionally tap the face or speak a greeting. No naming
demanded.

**Why it might work:** Rehearses the specific real-world *moment* rather than the abstract
face-name pair. Contextual reinstatement: the doorbell cue at practice matches the
doorbell cue at retrieval, which is one of the more reliable ways to get transfer. Frames
the encounter as pleasant anticipation, not a test.

**Failure mode:** The doorbell sound makes them get up and answer a door that nobody is
at, repeatedly — genuinely dangerous for a fall risk, and cruel. Probably needs a distinct
non-doorbell chime, or must be excluded for anyone with exit-seeking behaviour.

**Difficulty:** low · **Variant of:** Who's Coming Today

### 10. Warm-Up

**One line:** Twenty minutes before a real visit actually starts, the tablet quietly plays
three photos of the person who is on their way and one thing they might talk about.

**Input needed:** Nothing new — derived from the visitor's existing photos and the
calendar entry. Optionally one "conversation seed" from the visitor ("I'll bring the
puppy").

**Interaction:** No input at all. It plays by itself. Can be dismissed by ignoring it.

**Why it might work:** Straight priming, timed to the retrieval window rather than to the
daily session slot. The functional win is that the first ninety seconds of the visit go
well, which determines whether the visitor comes back — visitor attrition is one of the
strongest drivers of decline.

**Failure mode:** Interrupts something (a meal, a nap, a conversation) with an unrequested
noise. And it makes the app an intrusive presence rather than an invited one, which is a
different relationship than every other mechanic here.

**Difficulty:** medium · **Variant of:** Who's Coming Today

---

### 11. Tea and Toast

**One line:** The patient follows along with photos of their *own* kitchen, one step at a
time, to make a cup of tea — the kettle in their photo is their kettle, the mug is their
mug.

**Input needed:** 6–10 photos of the patient's actual kitchen, one per step, shot from
their standing position. Family confirms the sequence.

**Interaction:** Tap to advance, or watch on a timer. No "did you do it?" check.

**Why it might work:** Procedural memory and motor chaining are the last things to go.
Errorless step-by-step prompting with real environmental photos is a mainstream OT
technique (task breakdown / graded cueing) that here just gets a delivery mechanism.
Making tea is also enormously identity-laden for this generation — it's hosting, not
therapy.

**Failure mode:** Encourages an unsafe task. Kettles, hobs and scalds. Needs an explicit
family/OT sign-off per task and a hard exclusion list. Also the kitchen may have been
rearranged, or they may have moved, making every photo subtly wrong and the sequence
maddening.

**Difficulty:** medium · **Variant of:** The Way to the Bathroom

---

### 12. The Tablet Round

**One line:** At the time their medication is actually due, the patient sees a photo of
their own pill box in their own hand and a relative's voice saying "morning tablets, Dad —
the blue box."

**Input needed:** A photo of the patient's actual dosette box / blister pack in situ,
the times, and one short recorded prompt per time slot from a trusted voice.

**Interaction:** No input at all, or one tap on a large "done" picture (never a checkbox).

**Why it might work:** Prospective memory is the earliest and most functionally costly
casualty of dementia. Time-anchored external cueing with a familiar voice outperforms
alarms, which get ignored or misattributed. The real-object photo makes the target
unambiguous.

**Failure mode:** Double-dosing if "done" isn't reliable, or the patient becoming anxious
and distressed at an alarm they can't act on because the box is downstairs. Also puts the
app inside a clinical-safety boundary — this is the one mechanic that could kill someone,
and it must never be the *only* medication safeguard.

**Difficulty:** medium · **Variant of:** The Quiet Anchor

---

### 13. The Rehearsal Reel

**One line:** Over the six weeks before their granddaughter's wedding, a little of it
appears each day — the church, the dress, who'll be sitting next to them, what they'll be
asked — so that by the day itself it feels like something they already know.

**Input needed:** For an upcoming real event: 5–10 photos (venue, key people, the
patient's own outfit), a date, and 3–5 sentences of what will happen. Family adds these
progressively.

**Interaction:** Just watch, plus occasional two-picture recognition ("which one is the
church?").

**Why it might work:** Builds familiarity *before* the event so that the event itself is
recognition rather than a confusing novel environment — the same logic as pre-admission
hospital familiarisation. Distributed exposure over weeks is exactly the condition under
which even significantly impaired patients show learning. Also gives the patient something
to look *forward* to, which is a mood mechanism, not a memory one.

**Failure mode:** The event is cancelled, postponed, or the patient becomes too unwell to
attend — and they have been rehearsing it daily for six weeks. Also risks building
anticipatory anxiety rather than confidence in an anxious patient.

**Difficulty:** medium · **Variant of:** Who's Coming Today

### 14. Where I'm Sleeping Tonight

**One line:** The patient sees a photo of their own bed with their own quilt and their own
things on the nightstand, and hears their daughter say "that's your room, Mum, number
fourteen, along from the lounge."

**Input needed:** One photo of the patient's current bedroom taken from the doorway with
personal objects visible, room number/name, and one recorded orienting sentence from a
relative.

**Interaction:** Just watch. Available on demand from the always-present soft button.

**Why it might work:** "I want to go home" is very often "I want to feel safe," and a
substantial fraction of care-home distress and evening exit-seeking is not knowing where
one belongs tonight. Object recognition (their own quilt) is powerful and preserved. It
answers the fear without arguing with the belief.

**Failure mode:** The patient's belief is that home is their childhood house; showing them
a care-home room labelled "your room" is a contradiction of their reality and can trigger
exactly the grief and anger the app exists to avoid. The relative's script has to do a lot
of careful work here, and it should probably be gated behind the disclosure policy.

**Difficulty:** low · **Variant of:** The Way to the Bathroom

---

### 15. The Sundown Ritual

**One line:** As the light goes at the end of the day, the tablet does the same small
thing it always does — the same photo, the same song, the same voice saying goodnight —
and the patient just sits with it.

**Input needed:** One song the patient chose (or their family chose for them), one
photograph they love, and one 20-second goodnight recording. Sunset time is derived.

**Interaction:** No input at all.

**Why it might work:** Late-afternoon agitation ("sundowning") is a real and grinding
problem, and ritual predictability is one of the few things that reliably helps. Music
from ages 15–25 has exceptional autobiographical reach and can retrieve emotional state
even when nothing declarative survives. This is the mechanic that asks literally nothing
of the patient — its value is regulatory, not mnemonic.

**Failure mode:** Ritual becomes rigidity: any disruption (dead battery, staff turning it
off) then causes distress it wouldn't have caused before. And the wrong song, chosen by a
well-meaning relative, can land on a grief the family didn't know about.

**Difficulty:** low · **Variant of:** The Quiet Anchor

---

### 16. The Handover

**One line:** At the end of the session the patient is asked to *tell the app something* —
anything, into a big microphone button — and tomorrow that thing comes back to them, in
their own voice, as the first card of the day.

**Input needed:** Nothing from the family. Family optionally reviews the clips and can
promote a good one into the permanent library.

**Interaction:** Speak aloud into one very large picture-of-a-microphone button. Nothing
is transcribed, scored, or corrected. Silence is an acceptable answer and ends gracefully.

**Why it might work:** Reverses the role from tested to teller. Self-generation is the
strongest encoding manipulation known, and hearing one's own voice from yesterday is both
evidence of continuity and a self-cue. Motivationally, it converts the app from something
done *to* them into somewhere their voice is kept — which is close to the reason people
keep diaries.

**Failure mode:** They record something distressing, confused, or paranoid, and it is
played back to them the next morning. That is a serious harm and needs either family
review before replay or sentiment screening. Also they may record nothing for weeks and
experience the empty prompt as another failure.

**Difficulty:** medium · **Variant of:** novel

---

### 17. The Kept Promise

**One line:** A small card counts down the one thing they've been told is coming — "Tom
rings on Sunday" — and on Sunday it says "Tom rings today," and afterwards it says "Tom
rang."

**Input needed:** One recurring commitment per person from the family, and honesty about
keeping it. Nothing else.

**Interaction:** Just watch. One tap to see the person's face.

**Why it might work:** Almost all of the anxiety in mild-to-moderate dementia is
*not knowing what happens next*. A single reliable, externally-held commitment does more
for that than any amount of retrieval practice. The "afterwards" state closes the loop and
inoculates against "he never rings."

**Failure mode:** Tom doesn't ring. The card is then either a lie or a public record of
being let down, and both are worse than nothing. Needs a soft-fail state that is honest
without being an indictment, and family need to be warned that committing here is a real
commitment.

**Difficulty:** low · **Variant of:** Yesterday Actually Happened

---

### 18. Nothing Today

**One line:** On a day when the patient is clearly not up to it, the session becomes six
photographs of people they love, with names spoken, and nothing is asked of them at all.

**Input needed:** Nothing new — a fallback assembled from the highest-emotional-salience
content already in the library.

**Interaction:** No input at all. It plays and ends. Taps do nothing bad.

**Why it might work:** Attention and capacity fluctuate hour to hour, and the single
biggest cause of a patient refusing the app forever is one bad session where they felt
caught out. Having a floor below which the app *cannot* demand anything means a bad day
never becomes an abandonment. Passive viewing of loved faces still delivers social
presence and mood benefit.

**Failure mode:** Detecting "not up to it" wrongly, and dropping to the easiest mode for
someone who was fine — which is itself a demotion and readable as one. Must be invisible:
no "let's take it easy today" message, no visual difference in the shell.

**Difficulty:** medium · **Variant of:** The Quiet Anchor

---

### 19. The Song First

**One line:** A song from when they were twenty plays for fifteen seconds, and only then do
the photographs from that era appear — the music arrives before the faces, every time.

**Input needed:** Year of birth, 5–10 songs the family confirms mattered to them, and
photos tagged loosely by decade.

**Interaction:** Just watch and listen; optional tap-one-of-two on the photos afterwards.

**Why it might work:** Music-evoked autobiographical memory is unusually spared, and
priming with period music measurably opens access to material from that period. Using it
as a *pre-cue* rather than an activity in its own right means the music is doing
mechanistic work: warming the retrieval network before any demand lands on it.

**Failure mode:** Hearing loss makes the music an unpleasant distorted noise rather than a
cue. Songs carry grief — a first-dance song after a bereavement is a landmine. And a
15-second wait before anything visual happens may lose a patient with poor attention.

**Difficulty:** medium · **Variant of:** The Sundown Ritual

---

### 20. The Grief Weather Report

**One line:** *(Patient sees nothing.)* The patient's reaching-for-the-lost — how often
they pressed the heart button, what they asked for, at what time of day — quietly becomes
a picture the family and the clinical team can see.

**Input needed:** Nothing — derived entirely from The Standing Answer usage. Family opts
in explicitly and the patient's consent status is recorded.

**Interaction:** No input at all from the patient; this is a family/researcher surface.

**Why it might work:** Not a memory mechanic — an OT assessment instrument. Clusters of
searching behaviour at 4pm are actionable (change the routine, not the person). It also
lets the family revisit the disclosure policy on evidence rather than on their own
discomfort, which is the thing that usually drives it.

**Failure mode:** Surveillance of a person's grief without their meaningful consent. If
the patient ever discovers that pressing the heart button was being counted, the one
private thing in the app stops being private. There is a real argument this should not
exist; it's here because the phase is divergent.

**Difficulty:** low · **Variant of:** The Standing Answer

---

### 21. The Sentence They Can Keep

**One line:** One single sentence — chosen by the family as the most useful thing this
person could hold on to this month ("Jean comes on Wednesdays") — is asked back at
lengthening gaps across the session and across the week, with the answer always shown
first.

**Input needed:** One sentence per month, agreed between family and (ideally) an OT. That
is the entire input.

**Interaction:** Watch the answer, then say it or tap it back at 30s, 1m, 2m, 4m, 8m
intervals within the session, then across days.

**Why it might work:** This is spaced retrieval training almost unmodified from the
clinical protocol, which has the strongest evidence base of anything in this document for
mild-to-moderate dementia. The discipline is in the *single* target: the protocol works
because it doesn't try to teach ten things. Interval collapses immediately on any miss,
so error rate stays near zero.

**Failure mode:** Repetition within a single session is noticeable and can read as "you
keep forgetting, so I keep asking." The framing must make the app the one rehearsing, not
the patient being drilled. And a badly chosen sentence wastes a month of a life that has
few months to waste.

**Difficulty:** medium · **Variant of:** Pocket Answers

## Cross-cutting notes for the culling board

- Mechanics 2, 10, 12, 15, 18 require **zero patient input**. That set alone constitutes a
  usable product for late-stage patients and should not be treated as the low-value tail.
- Mechanics 4, 5, 11, 14 all depend on **photography of the patient's real environment**,
  which is a single shared onboarding burden on the family — build the capture flow once.
- Mechanics 1, 3, 10, 13, 17 all depend on **something in the real world actually
  happening as promised**. Every one of them has a failure mode that is worse than not
  having the feature. Treat "what happens when reality doesn't cooperate" as a
  first-class design problem, not an edge case.
- The disclosure policy (see framing) is not a setting on one mechanic. It is a
  cross-cutting product-level stance that touches 7, 8, 14, 20 and probably everything
  else eventually.
