# Recall Core — Mechanics Through the Memory Scientist's Lens

**Lens:** Every mechanic here is a different way of touching a memory trace. Some *probe* it (retrieval practice, the testing effect), some *feed* it (exposure, priming, errorless learning), some *route around* the damaged system entirely (procedural, musical, implicit memory). The organising principle is the retrieval-demand gradient: from **zero demand** (watch, listen, cannot fail) through **recognition** (preserved far longer than recall) through **cued recall** to **free recall** (which we almost never ask for, and never grade).

Two constraints from the science shape everything:

1. **Errorless learning beats trial-and-error in dementia.** Explicit memory can't tag an error as "wrong" and suppress it; the error gets encoded implicitly and *strengthens*. So we never let the patient guess into a void. Where a mechanic can produce a wrong answer, it must degrade gracefully into the right one before the moment closes.
2. **The temporal gradient is our friend.** Remote memories — especially those laid down in the reminiscence bump, roughly ages 10–30 — survive longest. Content should be weighted backwards in time, and any session that must escalate should escalate *from* the distant past *towards* the present, stopping invisibly wherever it gets hard.

A third, non-negotiable rule: **the app never shows a score to the patient.** Outcome data goes to the researcher; encouragement goes to the patient. There is no such thing as a failed session.

## 1. The Slow Album

- **oneLine:** Photographs from her life drift across the screen one at a time, unhurried, while her daughter's recorded voice says who is in each one and what was happening.
- **inputNeeded:** 10+ photos; for each, a 5–15 second voice clip from a relative naming the people and the occasion ("This is you and Dad at Whitby, the summer before Michael was born").
- **interaction:** None. It plays. One enormous on-screen button, always visible, that means "stay on this one longer."
- **whyItMightWork:** Pure errorless exposure — re-presentation of an autobiographical trace with no retrieval demand at all, so failure is structurally impossible. The narrating voice supplies social presence (a familiar prosody is itself a potent cue) and does the retrieval *for* her, which is re-consolidation without effort. Repeated spaced exposure across days strengthens the trace even absent conscious recollection.
- **failureMode:** A photograph of someone now dead, unlabelled as such, can land as fresh bereavement. Passive video is also easy to sleep through — engagement drops to zero and the family thinks it's "working" because sessions complete. Narration that is too explanatory ("that's your husband, you were married 40 years") can read as being told about your own life by a stranger.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** novel

## 2. The Answer First

- **oneLine:** A face appears with the name written large beneath it and spoken aloud, then a heartbeat later the name is gone and he is asked, gently, "and who is this?" — and if nothing comes, the name simply returns.
- **inputNeeded:** A clear face photo per person plus the name as text and as a spoken clip (relative's voice preferred over synthetic).
- **interaction:** Speak aloud, or tap a single large "show me" button. Both count as success.
- **whyItMightWork:** Textbook errorless learning at a near-zero retention interval. The retrieval attempt is engineered to succeed, so what gets encoded is the correct pairing, not an error. Even a "failed" attempt is followed immediately by the correct answer, so the trial ends in a correct state — which is the whole point of errorless protocols.
- **failureMode:** Even a one-second gap can feel like a test if the interface pauses expectantly. The word "who" is a demand word. If the name returns too fast it can feel like being corrected; too slow and it's an exam.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** novel

## 3. The Widening Gap

- **oneLine:** The same handful of faces and names come back through the session — first after a few seconds, then after a minute, then at the end — and each time she gets it, the gap before the next time grows.
- **inputNeeded:** Nothing new — derived from existing face/name content. Optionally the family marks 3–5 "priority" people (the carer, the daughter who visits, the ward nurse).
- **interaction:** Speak the name, or tap one of two large pictures. Never more than two.
- **whyItMightWork:** This is spaced retrieval training (Camp's expanding-interval protocol) — the single best-evidenced behavioural memory technique in dementia, and it works *because* it exploits intact procedural/implicit learning rather than damaged explicit encoding. The testing effect (retrieval strengthens more than restudy) applies even when the patient has no conscious sense of having practised. The interval collapses back on an error, so the trial always ends correct.
- **failureMode:** Repetition within one session can be experienced as being asked the same thing over and over — which is exactly the thing families find distressing about the disease, now performed by a machine. If the intervals visibly shrink after an error, an insightful patient reads it as demotion.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** The Answer First

## 4. The Fading Name

- **oneLine:** Under his brother's photograph the name is written out in full today; next week it says "Ge____"; the week after, just "G" — and he finds he can still say it.
- **inputNeeded:** Nothing new — derived from existing face/name content.
- **interaction:** Speak aloud, or tap to reveal the rest. Tapping is not a loss.
- **whyItMightWork:** The vanishing-cues method (Glisky). The cue is withdrawn only as fast as performance allows, so retrieval stays successful throughout — errorless, but with a genuinely increasing retrieval demand, which is what actually strengthens the trace. Letter fragments also act as phonological primes, engaging a partly spared route to the lexicon.
- **failureMode:** Requires reading, which excludes low-literacy and low-vision users and anyone with posterior cortical atrophy. Fluctuating day-to-day performance means the cue level can be wrong on a bad day, turning a designed success into a designed failure. Watching your own cue shrink is a visible progress bar — and it can go backwards.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** The Answer First

## 5. Coming Into Focus

- **oneLine:** A photograph starts as a soft blur and slowly sharpens; she can tap the moment she knows what it is, but it comes clear either way.
- **inputNeeded:** Any photo of a person, place or object from her life. Nothing else.
- **interaction:** One tap anywhere on the screen, at any time, or no tap at all.
- **whyItMightWork:** Perceptual priming and the pop-out of recognition — implicit perceptual memory is comparatively spared, so previously-seen images resolve at higher blur levels than novel ones. That gives a clean, non-verbal outcome measure the patient never perceives as a test. The subjective "aha" of resolution is intrinsically rewarding and is a felt experience of one's own memory working.
- **failureMode:** If it resolves into a face she doesn't recognise, the mechanic has just staged a slow reveal of her own deficit. Blur can also read simply as a broken screen ("it's out of focus, something's wrong with it") and provoke anxiety about the device rather than engagement.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** novel

## 6. This One or This One

- **oneLine:** Two large photographs, side by side: "which one is your sister Joan?" — and he touches one.
- **inputNeeded:** One labelled photo per family member. Foils are drawn automatically from other people already in the library or from a neutral stock set.
- **interaction:** Tap one of two large pictures. Targets fill half the screen each.
- **whyItMightWork:** Recognition memory is preserved substantially longer than free recall in AD — a two-alternative forced choice can still be at ceiling when the name is completely unavailable. Foil distance is the difficulty dial: a wildly dissimilar foil (different sex, different era, different hair) makes success near-certain, and narrowing that distance titrates difficulty without ever changing the interaction.
- **failureMode:** 50% correct by chance means a wrong answer is genuinely uninformative, but it does not *feel* uninformative to the person who gave it. Choosing the wrong face for your own sister, in front of a screen, is a small humiliation even with no one watching. Two-item displays also invite perseveration on one side.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** novel

## 7. Odd One In

- **oneLine:** Three kitchens appear, and she is asked, lightly, which one feels like hers — and one of them genuinely is.
- **inputNeeded:** Photos of real places from the patient's life (kitchen, garden, front door, church, workplace). Distractors are supplied by the app from era-matched stock imagery.
- **interaction:** Tap one of three large pictures.
- **whyItMightWork:** Trades on familiarity and the mere-exposure effect rather than recollection — the "feels like" framing licenses an implicit, feeling-based response, which is the system most likely to still be intact. Because the instruction asks about a feeling, no answer can be objectively wrong to the patient's own ears, while the data still cleanly separates hit from miss for the researcher.
- **failureMode:** The word "feels" only protects her until someone (a carer, a relative watching) says "no, that's not your kitchen." Era-mismatched stock photos make the real one trivially identifiable, which is a false success. Seeing your own former home among strangers' homes can trigger a wish to go there.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** This One or This One

## 8. Finish The Sentence

- **oneLine:** In her husband's voice: "On a Sunday, we always used to walk down to the..." — and the sentence just stops, waiting, and she says it, or she doesn't, and either way the sentence finishes itself.
- **inputNeeded:** 5–20 high-constraint sentence stems written by a relative about the patient's actual life, each with its ending, ideally recorded in a familiar voice.
- **interaction:** Speak aloud. No microphone required for the mechanic to work — a relative can mark it later, or it can simply move on.
- **whyItMightWork:** The generation effect: self-produced completions are retained far better than read ones. High-constraint stems (where only one ending fits) make production nearly automatic and lean on semantic and over-learned autobiographical routine rather than episodic detail — routines survive the specific episodes that formed them. The trailing prosody of a familiar voice is a powerful pull-cue.
- **failureMode:** Silence in a paused sentence is uniquely exposing — the interface is visibly waiting. If the stem is under-constrained, a reasonable answer gets treated as wrong. Hearing the voice of a spouse who has died can be devastating without warning and consent.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** The Fading Name

## 9. The Song That Was Ours

- **oneLine:** The first bars of a song from when he was twenty start playing, and he hums, or taps, or sings the words he still has, and the app sings the rest with him.
- **inputNeeded:** A list of 5–15 songs meaningful to the patient, ideally with the years they mattered; optionally a note of what each one is attached to ("their first dance").
- **interaction:** No input required. Optionally sing or tap along; optionally one large "play it again" button.
- **whyItMightWork:** Musical memory — particularly for melodies encoded before ~age 30 — is strikingly spared in AD, relying on medial prefrontal and motor circuits rather than the hippocampal system. Music-evoked autobiographical memory (MEAM) reliably retrieves episodic content that no verbal cue can reach, and produces genuine mood elevation independent of any recall. Singing along is procedural, not declarative: the words arrive as motor sequence, not as retrieval.
- **failureMode:** A song attached to grief will find the grief first. Volume and hearing loss make music aversive rather than moving. And a patient who once played or sang well and can no longer do so may experience the invitation to join in as a demonstration of loss.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium (licensing is the hard part, not the code)
- **variantOf:** novel

## 10. Hands Remember

- **oneLine:** The screen shows the shed, and the tools, and he does the thing he did ten thousand times — the same wiping motion, the same order of steps — and his hands know it before he does.
- **inputNeeded:** A description from the family of one over-learned lifelong skill or routine (a trade, an instrument, a card game, kneading, shaving, dealing, setting a table) plus a few photos of the real objects involved.
- **interaction:** Drag / repeated physical gesture on a large target zone, or a physical prop the app narrates alongside. Never a precision gesture.
- **whyItMightWork:** Procedural memory is subserved by basal ganglia and cerebellum, largely spared until late stages, and is *learnable* even in dense amnesia. This route asks nothing of the declarative system at all. It also restores competence and identity: the man who was a joiner for 45 years gets to be someone who is good at something, which is the motivational core the whole product is otherwise short of.
- **failureMode:** A degraded on-screen simulation of a skill someone genuinely mastered is insulting — a real carpenter knows a cartoon saw when he sees one. Tremor and arthritis make sustained gesture painful. Priming a lifelong work identity can also surface distress about no longer working.
- **difficultyToBuild:** high
- **variantOf:** novel

## 11. Back Along The Years

- **oneLine:** Each session starts a long way back — schooldays, courting, the first house — and walks gently forward through the decades, quietly turning around before it reaches the parts that have gone.
- **inputNeeded:** Existing photos tagged with an approximate year or decade. Family supplies dates, not new content.
- **interaction:** Whatever the underlying mechanic uses — this is a scheduling layer over the others.
- **whyItMightWork:** Directly instruments Ribot's temporal gradient: remote autobiographical memory survives disproportionately, and the reminiscence bump (ages ~10–30) is both the densest and the most emotionally positive stratum of autobiographical memory. Starting there guarantees an opening run of success, which sets expectancy and mood for the whole session, and the forward walk maps exactly where the gradient's edge currently sits — a genuinely useful longitudinal measure.
- **failureMode:** The turn-around must be invisible; if the session visibly stops at 1974 every time, the family can read the disease progression off the screen, and so can the patient. Also, the bump is not universally happy — for someone whose war, or whose loss, sits in that window, "start with your twenties" is the wrong instruction.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** novel

## 12. Which Came First

- **oneLine:** Two photographs of herself, decades apart, and she puts them in order — the young one and the older one — and is nearly always right.
- **inputNeeded:** 3+ photos of the same person across decades, with approximate years.
- **interaction:** Drag one picture above the other, or tap the earlier one. Tap version for tremor.
- **whyItMightWork:** Temporal-order judgement is a distinct memory operation from item memory and degrades separately, so it probes something the other mechanics miss. Crucially, wide-gap pairs can be solved on *perceptual* grounds (hair, clothing, photographic grain) even when episodic dating has gone entirely — so a patient can succeed via an intact route while the researcher still learns where genuine chronological judgement fails as the gap narrows.
- **failureMode:** Sustained side-by-side comparison of your young face and your old face is a confrontation with ageing that nobody asked for. Narrow gaps become guesswork fast. Dragging is a gesture many users in this population do not have.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** This One or This One

## 13. Who Told You That?

- **oneLine:** Two voices he knows have each told him something today, and later he is asked which one said "we're coming on Saturday."
- **inputNeeded:** 2+ short voice clips (5–10s) from *different* identifiable relatives, recorded the same week, each carrying a distinct piece of news.
- **interaction:** Tap one of two large photographs of the two speakers.
- **whyItMightWork:** Source memory (who/where/when a fact was acquired) is bound by prefrontal and hippocampal systems and degrades earlier and faster than item memory — so it is an unusually sensitive early marker, and it maps onto a real-world capability that matters enormously in daily life. Voice identity is also a partly separable, relatively durable channel.
- **failureMode:** This is the most likely mechanic in the set to produce failure, and failure here is close to the lived experience of the illness ("I can't remember who told me"). Source confusion is also the substrate of confabulation and of paranoid misattribution — being shown you got it wrong can seed genuine distress about being lied to. Handle as research instrument, not daily exercise.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** novel

## 14. The Room You Knew

- **oneLine:** Before anything is asked of her, the whole screen becomes the front room of the house on Ashfield Road, and it just sits there for half a minute while she settles.
- **inputNeeded:** 1–3 photographs (or a family-written description the app renders) of a place the patient lived in or spent long periods.
- **interaction:** No input at all. It is a preamble to whatever comes next.
- **whyItMightWork:** Encoding specificity and context reinstatement: retrieval is dramatically better when the environmental context of encoding is restored, and mental reinstatement works nearly as well as physical. Placing subsequent items in a reinstated context should lift performance on every mechanic that follows it — meaning it is a *multiplier*, not a standalone exercise. It also functions as a mood and arousal regulator before demand begins.
- **failureMode:** For someone with a wish-to-go-home agitation pattern, showing the old home is precisely the trigger to avoid, and can produce prolonged distress that outlasts the session. In a care home, a former home is a loaded image.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** novel

## 15. The Long Introduction

- **oneLine:** "This is Margaret. She's the one with the loud laugh — she taught you to drive in the church car park." Then, tomorrow, the same face, and the same two things about her.
- **inputNeeded:** Per person: face photo, name, and 2–3 distinctive, concrete, unchanging descriptors written by the family (a trait, a shared event, a physical feature).
- **interaction:** Just watch and listen; optionally repeat the name aloud.
- **whyItMightWork:** Levels-of-processing: elaborative, semantically rich encoding produces far more durable traces than rote name-face pairing, and face-name learning is notoriously the weakest form of paired-associate learning precisely because a name is arbitrary. Attaching a distinctive, meaningful attribute gives the retrieval system multiple routes in. Fixed wording across days adds the benefit of verbatim repetition.
- **failureMode:** Descriptors written carelessly become caricatures ("the fat one", "the one who never visits"). Being introduced to your own daughter, repeatedly, is a scenario that can wound if the framing is wrong — it must read as fond storytelling, not as briefing a stranger.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** The Slow Album

## 16. Say It Back

- **oneLine:** A voice says a name; he says it back; that's the whole exercise.
- **inputNeeded:** Nothing new — spoken names already in the library.
- **interaction:** Speak aloud. No scoring, no microphone strictly required.
- **whyItMightWork:** The production effect — saying a word aloud produces substantially better retention than reading or hearing it, via distinctive motor-articulatory encoding. It is also literally unfailable: the target is present at the moment of production, so this is rehearsal, not retrieval. Excellent as a warm-up: it establishes the vocal channel and a rhythm of success before any real demand.
- **failureMode:** Dysarthria, aphasia, or dentures make speaking aloud effortful or embarrassing, especially on a shared care-home tablet within earshot of others. Asked without framing it can feel like elocution drill, i.e. childlike.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** The Answer First

## 17. The Thing At Four O'Clock

- **oneLine:** In the morning the app says "your son is ringing at four" — and at ten past four it says, warmly, "did he ring?" and she says yes or no.
- **inputNeeded:** One real, genuinely-going-to-happen intention supplied by the family that day (a visit, a call, a tablet to take, the bins).
- **interaction:** Tap one of two large buttons, or say yes.
- **whyItMightWork:** Prospective memory (remembering to do a thing at a future time) is among the earliest and most functionally consequential capacities to fail, and it is almost entirely absent from paper-based cognitive testing. Anchoring the intention to a real event supplies an external event-based cue rather than a purely time-based one — the easier, more preservable form — and success here has direct daily-living value rather than merely test value.
- **failureMode:** The mechanic depends on relatives actually doing what they said. If the son doesn't ring, the app has manufactured a disappointment and possibly an argument. Reminding someone of an intention they then fail to act on is the definition of setting up a failure, and a "did you?" question invites a face-saving lie.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** novel

## 18. The Return Visit

- **oneLine:** A face he hasn't seen on the screen in four months comes back, introduced afresh as if for the first time — and it goes much faster than it did before.
- **inputNeeded:** Nothing new — the app retires content that has drifted out of reach and reintroduces it later.
- **interaction:** Whatever The Answer First uses.
- **whyItMightWork:** Savings in relearning (Ebbinghaus) — material that appears entirely lost is relearned faster than it was learned, demonstrating a residual trace below the threshold of recognition. Clinically this is one of very few ways to show that something survives in a patient who cannot recognise anything, which matters enormously to families. It also silently rescues content the schedule would otherwise abandon, so the library never visibly shrinks.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **failureMode:** Reintroducing a person the patient has genuinely lost can distress the family more than the patient — a relative watching their own mother be re-introduced to them is brutal. Retiring content at all requires a judgement about what is "lost", and getting that wrong removes people from someone's life by algorithm.
- **variantOf:** The Answer First

## 19. Talking Back

- **oneLine:** The screen shows a photograph and asks "what do you remember about this?" — and whatever she says is recorded and sent to her daughter, and nothing about it is marked or checked.
- **inputNeeded:** Photos with strong narrative potential (an event, a group, a place), plus family consent to receive recordings.
- **interaction:** Speak aloud into an always-open microphone; one large button to stop.
- **whyItMightWork:** Free recall, which is the most impaired capacity and therefore normally the cruellest to ask for — but here it is decoupled from evaluation entirely and reframed as *telling*, which is a socially valued act rather than a test. Narrative retrieval is generative and self-directed, engaging semantic and remote episodic content; and the output is genuinely valuable (families consistently rate recorded reminiscence as the most treasured artefact). Motivationally this may be the strongest mechanic in the set.
- **failureMode:** Open-ended questions produce long silences in patients with word-finding difficulty, and an open microphone recording that silence is grim. Confabulated or factually wrong accounts get sent to family, who may correct them. Anything said in distress or anger is also recorded and transmitted — consent and deletion must be trivially easy, and they won't be.
- **difficultyToBuild:** medium
- **variantOf:** Finish The Sentence

## 20. The Photograph That Waits

- **oneLine:** Between sessions the tablet just sits on the sideboard showing one photograph from her life, changing quietly a few times a day, and nobody ever asks her about it.
- **inputNeeded:** Nothing new — existing photo library.
- **interaction:** No input at all, ever. Not part of a session.
- **whyItMightWork:** Incidental encoding and mere exposure — repeated non-attended presentation increases familiarity and processing fluency without any intentional learning, and implicit familiarity is among the last things to go. It multiplies total exposure time by an order of magnitude over what a 10-minute daily session can deliver, and it converts the device from a thing that asks her questions into a thing that shows her her own life. That reframing alone may do more for adherence than any mechanic here.
- **failureMode:** An unattended screen showing the dead is an ambush with no operator present. In a care home a shared device would be broadcasting one resident's private life to the room. Burn-in of the device into background furniture also means exposure may drop to genuinely zero while looking like coverage.
- **difficultyToBuild:** low
- **variantOf:** The Slow Album

## Cross-cutting notes for the culling board

- **The no-fail set** (1, 5, 9, 14, 16, 20, and arguably 10) can be run as a complete product on its own for someone in moderate-to-severe stages. Every one of them is exposure, priming or procedure rather than test. If the board cuts hard, cut *towards* this set for late-stage users and keep the retrieval mechanics for MCI/early-stage.
- **The two genuinely evidence-backed mechanics** are #3 (spaced retrieval, Camp) and #4 (vanishing cues, Glisky) — these have the most literature behind them. #9 (music) has the strongest affective evidence.
- **The two most dangerous** are #13 (source memory) and #17 (prospective memory). Both are excellent instruments and both manufacture failure by design. Consider restricting them to researcher-initiated assessment sessions rather than daily use.
- **#11 and #14 are not exercises, they are modifiers.** They wrap the others. Costing them as standalone mechanics will undervalue them.
- **Foil selection is the single highest-leverage difficulty control in the whole system** (#6, #7, #12) and it is invisible to the patient. One dial, no interface change, full titration from ceiling to floor.
